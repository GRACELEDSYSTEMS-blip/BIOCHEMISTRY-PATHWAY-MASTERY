What Are Lipids? — The Two Main Families

Lipids are a diverse group of biological molecules united by one property: they are hydrophobic (water-repelling). They don't dissolve in water — which is exactly why they are so useful as membranes, energy stores, and signalling molecules. The two main structural families are acyl-lipids and isoprenoids.

Fatty Acids — Structure and Nomenclature

Fatty acids are the building blocks of most lipids. They are amphipathic molecules — they have a polar carboxyl group (–COOH) at one end that interacts with water, and a long non-polar hydrocarbon tail that repels water. The tail is where the energy is stored — all those C–H bonds can be oxidised to release energy.

Fatty acids are classified by three features:

Chain length: Very long chain (>20C) · Long chain (C12–C20) · Medium chain (C6–C12) · Short chain (C4). This matters because different enzymes handle different chain lengths, and chain length determines how the fat is processed and transported.

Degree of unsaturation: Saturated (no double bonds, maximum H) · Monounsaturated (one double bond) · Polyunsaturated (multiple double bonds). Double bonds reduce energy yield slightly because fewer C–H bonds are present to oxidise.

Position of double bonds: Omega (ω) notation counts from the methyl end of the chain. Omega-3 fatty acids (first double bond at carbon 3 from the end) are considered health-promoting — they reduce inflammation and support cardiovascular health.

🔤 Reading Fatty Acid Shorthand
C18:1 Δ9 (Oleic acid) — 18 carbons total, 1 double bond, positioned at carbon 9 counting from the carboxyl end (between C9 and C10).

Omega (ω) notation — counts from the methyl end (the tail). Oleic acid = 18:1ω9. Linoleic acid = 18:2Δ9,12 = 18:2ω6,9.

Essential fatty acids: Mammals lack the enzymes to introduce double bonds beyond C-9. So linoleate (18:2 Δ9,12), linolenate (18:3 Δ9,12,15), and arachidonate (20:4 Δ5,8,11,14) must come from the diet — they are essential fatty acids.

Triglycerides — The Main Energy Store and How They're Mobilised

Triglycerides (triacylglycerols, TAGs) are the body's primary form of stored energy. Each TAG molecule is made of glycerol + 3 fatty acids esterified to it. They are stored in adipose tissue (fat cells), which are essentially specialised containers for fat droplets. Fat is a far superior energy store to glycogen — 9 kcal/g vs 4 kcal/g — and fat stores are essentially unlimited, while glycogen is limited to ~400g in total.

Mobilisation during fasting: When blood glucose falls and glucagon/epinephrine rise, Hormone-Sensitive Lipase (HSL) in adipose tissue is activated via the cAMP cascade (glucagon/epinephrine → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA → HSL). HSL hydrolyses TAGs into free fatty acids (FFAs) and glycerol. FFAs enter the blood, travel to peripheral tissues, and are oxidised by beta-oxidation. Glycerol travels to the liver where it is converted to DHAP and enters gluconeogenesis.

Dietary fat mobilisation: Dietary fats must be emulsified by bile salts in the small intestine to make them accessible to pancreatic lipase. Free fatty acids (FFAs) stimulate the duodenum to release Cholecystokinin (CCK / Pancreozymin) — a 33-amino acid hormone that empties the gallbladder (more bile salts) and triggers pancreatic enzyme release. TAGs are broken down into FFAs and glycerol, absorbed into intestinal cells, re-packaged into chylomicrons (with proteins), and released into the lymph → blood. In tissues, Lipoprotein Lipase hydrolyses chylomicrons for uptake into adipocytes or hepatic cells.

🔑
Glycerol from TAG hydrolysis enters glycolysis as DHAP — it can be used for energy or for gluconeogenesis in the liver. Fatty acids cannot cross the blood-brain barrier and cannot be converted to glucose (even-chain FAs) — they are oxidised purely for energy in peripheral tissues.
Getting Fatty Acids INTO the Mitochondria — The Carnitine Shuttle

Beta-oxidation takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. But activated fatty acids (fatty acyl-CoA) cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane on their own — CoA is too large to pass through. The solution is a carnitine carrier system that swaps the CoA for carnitine, shuttles the fatty acid across, and swaps back.

This 3-step process is the rate-limiting step for beta-oxidation — controlling entry into the pathway controls the whole pathway.

1
Activation — Fatty acid + CoA → Fatty Acyl-CoA (cytosol)
Before the fatty acid can be transported, it must first be activated by esterification with Coenzyme A. This is catalysed by Acyl-CoA Synthetase (Thiokinase) on the outer mitochondrial membrane or ER. The reaction requires ATP (consumed as ATP → AMP + PPi, equivalent to 2 ATP), and PPi is immediately hydrolysed to 2Pi by pyrophosphatase, driving the reaction irreversibly forward. The product, Fatty Acyl-CoA, is the activated form — it is "charged" and ready for oxidation. Different chain lengths have different Acyl-CoA Synthetases.
2
CPT-I — Transfer to Carnitine at the Outer Membrane
Carnitine Palmitoyl Transferase I (CPT-I) sits on the cytosolic surface of the outer mitochondrial membrane. It transfers the fatty acid from CoA onto carnitine (replacing CoA with carnitine), forming acylcarnitine. This is the key regulated step — CPT-I is inhibited by malonyl-CoA (the first committed intermediate of fatty acid synthesis). When fatty acid synthesis is active (fed state, high malonyl-CoA), beta-oxidation is automatically shut off. This prevents the futile cycle of simultaneously making and breaking down fatty acids.
3
Antiporter — Acylcarnitine crosses the inner membrane
A specific antiporter in the inner mitochondrial membrane exchanges acylcarnitine (moving inward) for free carnitine (moving outward). One goes in as the other comes out — hence "antiporter." This is a 1:1 exchange. The acylcarnitine is now inside the matrix.
4
CPT-II — Transfer back to CoA in the Matrix
Carnitine Palmitoyl Transferase II (CPT-II) sits on the matrix face of the inner mitochondrial membrane. It transfers the fatty acid back from carnitine onto a new molecule of CoA (matrix CoA), regenerating Fatty Acyl-CoA inside the matrix. Free carnitine is released and transported back out via the antiporter for reuse. The fatty acyl-CoA is now in the matrix — ready for beta-oxidation.
⚡
Short and medium chain fatty acids (C4–C12) do NOT need the carnitine shuttle — they can diffuse directly across the inner mitochondrial membrane. This is why medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) formulas are used in patients with CPT-I or CPT-II deficiency — the fatty acids bypass the blocked transport system entirely.
💡 Beta-oxidation is called "beta" because oxidation occurs at the beta carbon (C-3) — the second carbon from the carboxyl end. Each cycle removes 2 carbons as acetyl-CoA and produces 1 FADH₂ + 1 NADH. Tap any card to expand.

CoA attacks the C-3 carbon in a thiolytic cleavage — the bond between C-2 and C-3 is broken. One CoA molecule is consumed per cycle to form the new acetyl-CoA. The shortened acyl-CoA then undergoes another round of beta-oxidation. For palmitate (16C), this cycle runs 7 times to produce 8 acetyl-CoA molecules (the last cycle yields 2 acetyl-CoA simultaneously).

TCA Cycle ConnectionAcetyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle via Citrate Synthase (OAA + Acetyl-CoA → Citrate). Each acetyl-CoA yields 3 NADH + 1 FADH₂ + 1 GTP = ~10 ATP. All 8 acetyl-CoAs from palmitate produce 80 ATP (old: 80 ATP; new: ~82.5 ATP).
Special Cases — Odd-Chain, Unsaturated, Branched-Chain
Standard beta-oxidation handles even-chain saturated FAs. Special enzymes handle the rest.
ODD
Odd-Chain Fatty Acids → Propionyl-CoA → Succinyl-CoA
Final product: Propionyl-CoA (3C) → via 3 enzymes → Succinyl-CoA → TCA cycle
Requires Biotin + Vit B₁₂
▼
Odd-chain fatty acids (found in some bacteria, plants, and marine organisms, as well as in the human colon) undergo normal beta-oxidation until the final thiolase cleavage — which produces a 3-carbon Propionyl-CoA instead of two acetyl-CoAs. Propionyl-CoA cannot enter the TCA cycle directly and requires three specific enzymes:

1. Propionyl-CoA Carboxylase (requires Biotin as cofactor): adds CO₂ to Propionyl-CoA → D-Methylmalonyl-CoA (uses ATP). This is analogous to Pyruvate Carboxylase — biotin is the CO₂ carrier in both.

2. Methylmalonyl-CoA Epimerase: converts D-Methylmalonyl-CoA → L-Methylmalonyl-CoA (stereoisomeric conversion).

3. Methylmalonyl-CoA Mutase (requires Vitamin B₁₂ (adenosylcobalamin) as cofactor): rearranges L-Methylmalonyl-CoA → Succinyl-CoA. Succinyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle directly as a 4-carbon intermediate. This is the only way mammals convert fat carbons into glucose (odd-chain fatty acid carbons enter TCA at succinyl-CoA → OAA → gluconeogenesis).
💡
Vitamin B₁₂ deficiency impairs Methylmalonyl-CoA Mutase → methylmalonyl-CoA accumulates → elevated methylmalonic acid in urine (methylmalonic aciduria). This is one of the diagnostic markers for B₁₂ deficiency, alongside homocystinuria.
UNS
Unsaturated Fatty Acids — Extra Enzymes Needed
Cis double bonds bypass Step 1; resolved by Enoyl-CoA Isomerase or 2,4-Dienoyl-CoA Reductase
2 Extra Enzymes
▼
The problem with unsaturated fatty acids is that their natural double bonds are cis, but beta-oxidation step 1 (Acyl-CoA Dehydrogenase) produces a trans double bond. When the cycle reaches the position of a pre-existing cis double bond in the chain, it cannot form the required trans-Δ2 configuration — the geometry is wrong for Enoyl-CoA Hydratase (step 2).

Two additional enzymes resolve this:

Enoyl-CoA Isomerase: handles monounsaturated fatty acids. Converts the cis-Δ3 enoyl-CoA to trans-Δ2 enoyl-CoA, which is then the correct substrate for step 2 (Hydratase). One fewer FADH₂ is produced per double bond already present (step 1 is bypassed).

2,4-Dienoyl-CoA Reductase (requires NADPH): handles polyunsaturated fatty acids where two adjacent double bonds create a 2,4-dienoyl intermediate. Reduces it to trans-Δ3-enoyl-CoA, which Enoyl-CoA Isomerase then converts to the correct trans-Δ2 form. One FADH₂ and one NADPH are lost for each double bond pair handled this way — which is why unsaturated FAs yield slightly less ATP than saturated FAs of the same length.
BCH
Branched-Chain & Very Long Chain FA — Peroxisomal Oxidation
Very long chain FAs → peroxisomal beta-oxidation → octanoyl-CoA → mitochondrial beta-oxidation
PeroxisomesRefsum's Disease
▼
Why peroxisomes? Very long chain fatty acids (>C20), branched-chain fatty acids, some prostaglandins, and leukotrienes are too large or too complex to enter mitochondria via the carnitine shuttle. They undergo initial oxidation in peroxisomes using the same enzymatic steps as mitochondrial beta-oxidation, until the chain is shortened to octanoyl-CoA (C8). At that point, the chain is small enough to transfer to mitochondria for completion.

Phytanic acid and Refsum's Disease: Phytanic acid is a branched-chain fatty acid derived from the breakdown of chlorophyll (found in plant foods and meat from plant-eating animals). It cannot undergo direct beta-oxidation because of the methyl branch at C-3 (which would block thiolase). Instead, phytanic acid first undergoes alpha-oxidation in peroxisomes — an alpha-hydroxylase removes one carbon (as CO₂), producing pristanic acid. Pristanic acid can then undergo beta-oxidation normally.

Refsum's Disease (Adult Refsum's Disease): Rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by deficiency of phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase (the alpha-hydroxylase). Phytanic acid accumulates in tissues — causing nerve damage, retinal degeneration (retinitis pigmentosa), ataxia (spastic movement), bone and skin changes. Treatment: strict avoidance of chlorophyll-containing foods (green vegetables) and meat from plant-eating animals.
How We Count ATP from Beta-Oxidation

Calculating the ATP yield from fatty acid oxidation is a favourite exam topic — and it requires combining the yield from beta-oxidation itself with the yield from the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation for each acetyl-CoA produced. Here we use palmitate (16-carbon, fully saturated) as the worked example — it is the most common calculation asked in exams.

The old vs new ATP controversy: Your lecturer's slides show both the classical values (NADH = 3 ATP, FADH₂ = 2 ATP, giving 106 ATP for palmitate) and the modern P/O ratio values (NADH = 2.5 ATP, FADH₂ = 1.5 ATP, giving ~108 ATP). You need to know both — know which one your lecturer expects in exams.

🔢 The Logic of the Calculation
Think of it in three layers:
Layer 1 — Beta-oxidation: Each cycle produces 1 FADH₂ + 1 NADH. Palmitate (16C) needs 7 cycles = 7 FADH₂ + 7 NADH.
Layer 2 — TCA cycle: Each acetyl-CoA produces 3 NADH + 1 FADH₂ + 1 GTP. 8 acetyl-CoA × 10 ATP = 80 ATP.
Layer 3 — Subtract activation cost: Activation used ATP → AMP = 2 ATP equivalents.
Total = beta-oxidation ATP + TCA ATP − 2.

Palmitate + CoA + ATP → AMP + PPi → 2 Pi
ATP is cleaved to AMP (not ADP), so this costs 2 ATP equivalents. PPi hydrolysis is irreversible and drives the reaction forward.

Products: 8 Acetyl-CoA (one released per cycle; the last cycle yields the final 2 simultaneously)
Step 3 — TCA Cycle (8 Acetyl-CoA)

8 × (3 NADH + 1 FADH₂ + 1 GTP) = 24 NADH + 8 FADH₂ + 8 GTP

🔑
Why does fat yield so much more energy than glucose? Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is already partially oxidised — it contains oxygen atoms. Fatty acids are highly reduced hydrocarbons — almost all carbons are bonded to hydrogen. The more reduced a molecule, the more energy is released when it is fully oxidised. This is why fat at 9 kcal/g yields more than double the energy of carbohydrate at 4 kcal/g.

What Are Ketone Bodies and Why Do We Make Them?

Ketone bodies are water-soluble molecules made in the liver from excess Acetyl-CoA during fasting, starvation, prolonged exercise, or uncontrolled diabetes. They are the liver's way of sending fat-derived fuel to the rest of the body — particularly the brain, which cannot use fatty acids directly.

Here is the fundamental scenario: during fasting or carbohydrate starvation, OAA (oxaloacetate) in the liver is depleted because gluconeogenesis uses it to make glucose. OAA is the entry point for Acetyl-CoA into the TCA cycle — without enough OAA, Acetyl-CoA cannot enter the TCA cycle even though it is being produced in large quantities from beta-oxidation. The TCA cycle is throttled by OAA depletion, and Acetyl-CoA backs up. The liver diverts this excess Acetyl-CoA into ketone body synthesis instead.

🏭 The Overflow Factory Analogy
Imagine a factory (liver) that converts raw material (fatty acids) into a product (Acetyl-CoA). Normally, the production line (TCA cycle) processes everything. But during fasting, the production line slows (OAA depleted) while raw material input (fatty acid mobilisation) increases. The factory is overproducing faster than it can process. So the manager (liver) packages the excess into portable, water-soluble containers (ketone bodies) and ships them out to other factories (brain, heart, muscle) that can use them as fuel. This is ketogenesis.

Why can't the brain use fatty acids? Fatty acids are too large and too tightly bound to albumin to cross the blood-brain barrier. The brain has very low capacity for fatty acid oxidation. But ketone bodies are small and water-soluble — they cross the blood-brain barrier easily and can be metabolised by neurons. During prolonged fasting, the brain shifts from 100% glucose to using ketone bodies for up to 70% of its energy needs, dramatically reducing the demand for gluconeogenesis and protecting muscle protein from breakdown.

Ketone Body Utilisation — How Peripheral Tissues Use Them

Liver makes ketone bodies but cannot use them itself — it lacks Succinyl-CoA:Acetoacetate CoA Transferase (Thiophorase), the enzyme needed for ketone body activation in peripheral tissues. This ensures ketone bodies are exported to where they are needed. Brain, heart, skeletal muscle, and kidney can all use ketone bodies.

Succinyl-CoA:Acetoacetate CoA Transferase (Thiophorase) transfers CoA from Succinyl-CoA (a TCA intermediate) to acetoacetate, producing Acetoacetyl-CoA. This "costs" one CoA equivalent from the TCA cycle but is energetically acceptable. This enzyme is absent in the liver — which is why the liver cannot use its own ketone bodies.
3
Acetoacetyl-CoA → 2 Acetyl-CoA (Thiolase)
Thiolase cleaves Acetoacetyl-CoA into 2 Acetyl-CoA, which enter the TCA cycle normally → ATP generation. The net yield of ketone body oxidation is similar to that of glucose — giving the brain an efficient alternative fuel during prolonged starvation.
Plain English — The Ketone Body System
The liver acts as a ketone body factory — it converts excess fat-derived fuel into a portable, water-soluble, brain-accessible form. The rest of the body acts as a ketone body consumer — converting them back to Acetyl-CoA for the TCA cycle. The liver is the only producer; it cannot consume its own product. This clever division of labour ensures that during fasting, the brain gets the fuel it needs without requiring the liver to give up glucose that could be used elsewhere.
⚕️ Clinical disorders of fatty acid oxidation and ketone metabolism are united by one theme: when fat cannot be oxidised normally, the body cannot switch from glucose to fat during fasting — leading to energy failure, organ damage, or dangerous metabolite accumulation.
MCAD Deficiency — Medium-Chain Acyl-CoA Dehydrogenase Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: MCAD (Step 1 of beta-oxidation for C6–C12 fatty acids)
Most common FA oxidation defectAutosomal RecessiveNewborn Screen detectable

MCAD deficiency is one of the most common inborn errors of metabolism — affecting approximately 1 in 10,000–15,000 live births in Western populations. Medium-chain fatty acids (C6–C12) cannot be oxidised because the first dehydrogenation step is blocked.

Why is it dangerous during fasting? During fasting, the body normally shifts from glucose to fat oxidation. In MCAD deficiency, medium-chain fatty acids back up and cannot be converted to Acetyl-CoA. Two consequences: (1) hypoketotic hypoglycaemia — blood glucose falls (fat oxidation failing) but ketone bodies are not produced (no Acetyl-CoA from medium-chain fats for ketogenesis) — the brain has neither glucose nor ketones; (2) accumulating medium-chain fatty acids and their derivatives are directly toxic to the liver and brain.

Presentation: Previously well infant develops acute encephalopathy, vomiting, and hypoglycaemia triggered by an illness (viral infection) that causes a period of reduced feeding. Can be mistaken for Reye syndrome. Without diagnosis, can be rapidly fatal.

Key lab finding: Elevated C8 (octanoyl) acylcarnitines on tandem mass spectrometry. This is why MCAD is included in newborn screening — early detection and simple dietary management (avoid prolonged fasting) is life-saving.

Treatment: Avoid fasting. Frequent carbohydrate feeds. Emergency dextrose infusion during illness. Carnitine supplementation may help clear toxic acylcarnitines.
Refsum's Disease (Adult Refsum's Disease — ARD)
Deficient enzyme: Phytanoyl-CoA Hydroxylase (alpha-hydroxylase in peroxisomes)
Autosomal RecessivePeroxisomal DisorderTreatable by diet

Phytanic acid is a branched-chain fatty acid formed from the breakdown of phytol (a component of chlorophyll). Because of the methyl branch at C-3, phytanic acid cannot undergo direct beta-oxidation — it first requires alpha-oxidation in peroxisomes to remove one carbon. In Refsum's disease, the alpha-hydroxylase is deficient, so phytanic acid accumulates in all tissues.

Clinical features: Phytanic acid accumulation causes progressive damage to multiple systems:
— Peripheral neuropathy (nerve damage — weakness, sensory loss)
— Retinitis pigmentosa (progressive retinal degeneration → night blindness → eventual vision loss)
— Cerebellar ataxia (coordination problems, spastic movement)
— Anosmia (loss of smell)
— Cardiac arrhythmias
— Ichthyosis (scaly skin)
— Bone changes (shortening of metatarsal bones)

Treatment: Strict dietary restriction of phytanic acid — avoid all green vegetables (chlorophyll source), meat from ruminants (cows, sheep store phytanic acid in fat), and dairy products from ruminants. With strict dietary control, disease progression can be halted and symptoms may partially improve.
Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)
Mechanism: Insulin deficiency → uncontrolled lipolysis + unopposed ketogenesis → ketone accumulation → acidosis
Type 1 DM primarilyMedical EmergencyHigh Anion Gap Acidosis

The biochemical chain: In insulin deficiency (Type 1 DM or severe Type 2), glucose cannot enter cells. The body interprets this as starvation — glucagon rises, epinephrine rises, Hormone-Sensitive Lipase is maximally activated → massive lipolysis → FFAs flood into the liver. Beta-oxidation runs at maximum rate → enormous Acetyl-CoA production. OAA is depleted (gluconeogenesis maximal). Acetyl-CoA cannot enter TCA cycle → diverted entirely to ketogenesis → acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate accumulate in blood at pathological levels (normal <0.1 mmol/L; DKA >3 mmol/L).

Why acidosis? Acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate are strong acids (pKa ~3.5–4.7). At high concentrations they overwhelm the body's buffering systems → blood pH falls below 7.3. This impairs haemoglobin's ability to bind oxygen, disrupts enzyme function, and can cause coma and death.

Clinical triad of DKA: Hyperglycaemia (usually >11 mmol/L), Ketonaemia/Ketonuria, Metabolic acidosis (pH <7.3, bicarb <15 mmol/L).

Symptoms: Fruity breath (acetone), Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid breathing — compensatory respiratory alkalosis), nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, dehydration, confusion, coma.

Treatment: IV fluids (correct dehydration), Insulin infusion (suppress lipolysis and ketogenesis, promote glucose uptake), Electrolyte replacement (especially K⁺ — insulin drives K⁺ into cells, can cause hypokalaemia). Bicarbonate only for severe acidosis (pH <6.9).
CPT-I and CPT-II Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: Carnitine Palmitoyl Transferase I or II — blocks fatty acid entry into mitochondria
Autosomal RecessiveLong-chain FA cannot be oxidised

CPT-I Deficiency: The transfer of long-chain fatty acids from CoA to carnitine is blocked. Long-chain FA accumulate in the cytosol. Beta-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids is completely blocked. Presents with hypoketotic hypoglycaemia during fasting (similar to MCAD) + elevated free carnitine (backed up, not esterified). The liver is specifically affected (hepatomegaly, liver dysfunction). The key distinguishing feature: elevated free carnitine with low acylcarnitines. Paradoxically, medium and short-chain fatty acids can still be oxidised (they don't need CPT-I).

CPT-II Deficiency: The reconstitution of fatty acyl-CoA in the matrix is blocked. Three clinical forms: severe neonatal (fatal cardiomyopathy + hypoglycaemia), infantile (liver disease + hypoketotic hypoglycaemia), and adult myopathic form (exercise-induced rhabdomyolysis — similar presentation to McArdle disease). The adult form is the most common and presents with muscle pain + myoglobinuria after prolonged exercise, fasting, or cold exposure.

Treatment: Avoid fasting. Frequent carbohydrate-rich meals. Supplemental medium-chain triglycerides (MCT oil) — MCTs bypass CPT-I and II entirely, reaching the matrix directly without carnitine.

⚠️
Three types of diabetic coma: (1) Hypoglycaemic coma — blood sugar too low (too much insulin). (2) Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) — hyperglycaemia + ketoacidosis + dehydration (mainly Type 1). (3) Hyperosmolar Non-Ketotic Coma (HONK) — extremely high blood sugar (>33 mmol/L) causing profound osmotic dehydration without significant ketosis (mainly Type 2 — enough insulin to suppress ketogenesis but not enough to prevent hyperglycaemia).

Assertion
Odd-chain fatty acid oxidation can contribute carbons to gluconeogenesis, unlike even-chain fatty acid oxidation.
Reason
The terminal propionyl-CoA from odd-chain FA oxidation is converted to succinyl-CoA via biotin-dependent and vitamin B12-dependent enzymes, entering the TCA cycle and exiting as oxaloacetate for gluconeogenesis.
Both correct. Even-chain FAs produce only acetyl-CoA - both carbons lost as CO2 per TCA turn, gluconeogenically inert. Odd-chain FAs produce propionyl-CoA from their last 3 carbons. Propionyl-CoA Carboxylase (biotin) and Methylmalonyl-CoA Mutase (vitamin B12) convert it to succinyl-CoA, a TCA intermediate contributing to OAA and gluconeogenesis. The only way fatty acid carbons can enter gluconeogenesis in mammals.
Assertion
Ketone bodies are produced in the liver but cannot be used by the liver as an energy source.
Reason
The liver lacks Succinyl-CoA:Acetoacetate CoA Transferase (Thiophorase), the enzyme required to activate acetoacetate for oxidation in peripheral tissues.
Both correct. The liver synthesises ketone bodies from excess acetyl-CoA during fasting. However, the liver lacks thiophorase - the enzyme that transfers CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate to form acetoacetyl-CoA. Without this enzyme, the liver cannot activate its own ketone bodies for oxidation. This ensures ketone bodies are exported to brain, heart, and muscles that do possess thiophorase.
Assertion
Short and medium chain fatty acids do not require the carnitine shuttle to enter the mitochondria.
Reason
Short and medium chain fatty acids can diffuse directly across the inner mitochondrial membrane.
Both correct. Long-chain FAs (>C12) require CPT-I/carnitine/CPT-II shuttle. Short (C4) and medium-chain (C6-C12) FAs are small and lipophilic enough to diffuse directly across the inner membrane. This is clinically exploited in CPT deficiency - MCT oil provides fatty acids that bypass the blocked transporter.
Assertion
In diabetic ketoacidosis, insulin deficiency leads to massive ketone body production causing metabolic alkalosis.
Reason
Acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate are strong acids that when produced in excess consume the bicarbonate buffer and lower blood pH.
The assertion is FALSE - DKA causes metabolic ACIDOSIS not alkalosis. The reason is TRUE and correctly explains why acidosis occurs. Assertion false, reason true = D.
Assertion
During fasting, the liver simultaneously undergoes active gluconeogenesis and active fatty acid oxidation.
Reason
Acetyl-CoA from fatty acid oxidation activates Pyruvate Carboxylase generating oxaloacetate for gluconeogenesis, while NADH from beta-oxidation provides energy for gluconeogenic reactions.
Both correct. During fasting, glucagon activates lipolysis providing fatty acids for beta-oxidation while promoting gluconeogenesis. Acetyl-CoA from beta-oxidation allosterically activates Pyruvate Carboxylase (pyruvate + CO2 to OAA feeding gluconeogenesis) and NADH helps drive energetically costly gluconeogenic reactions.
