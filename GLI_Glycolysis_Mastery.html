Glycolysis Mastery
The universal pathway of glucose breakdown. Ten enzymatic steps convert one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, capturing energy as ATP and NADH. Occurs in the cytosol of virtually every living cell.

Enzymatic Steps

ATP Produced

Location
Cytosol of all cells. No mitochondria required — this is why red blood cells, which lack mitochondria, rely entirely on glycolysis for ATP.
Overall Equation
Glucose + 2 NAD⁺ + 2 ADP + 2 Pᵢ
→ 2 Pyruvate + 2 NADH + 2 H⁺ + 2 ATP + 2 H₂O
Irreversible Steps
Steps 1, 3, and 10 are irreversible under physiological conditions. These are the three regulated steps and the points that differ in gluconeogenesis.
Aerobic vs. Anaerobic
Glycolysis itself is oxygen-independent. Aerobically, pyruvate enters the Krebs cycle. Anaerobically, it is reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD⁺.
Energy Investment Phase Steps 1–5 · Costs 2 ATP
Glucose is phosphorylated and split into two three-carbon molecules. Two ATP are consumed in steps 1 and 3. The purpose is to destabilise and commit the glucose molecule so that energy can be extracted in the second phase. Think of this as the cost of entry — you spend before you earn.
Energy Payoff Phase Steps 6–10 · Produces 4 ATP + 2 NADH
Each of the two three-carbon molecules goes through steps 6–10, so every yield is doubled. Four ATP are produced by substrate-level phosphorylation (steps 7 and 10 × 2), and two NADH are produced at step 6 × 2. Net gain: 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose.

Glucose → Glucose-6-phosphate

Explanation
Hexokinase phosphorylates glucose using one ATP, producing Glucose-6-phosphate. It has a high affinity for glucose (low Km) and is found in most tissues. It is subject to product inhibition — when G6P accumulates, hexokinase is inhibited, preventing wasteful ATP consumption.

In the liver and pancreatic β-cells, Glucokinase (Hexokinase IV) performs this step instead. Glucokinase has a low affinity for glucose (high Km) and is not inhibited by G6P — it acts as a glucose sensor, only active when blood glucose is high (e.g. after a meal).

Inter-pathway link: Pentose Phosphate PathwayG6P is the entry point of the Pentose Phosphate Pathway, producing NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate for biosynthesis and antioxidant defence. Glycogen SynthesisG6P can be converted to G1P by phosphoglucomutase and then incorporated into glycogen for storage. GluconeogenesisG6P is dephosphorylated by Glucose-6-phosphatase (present only in liver and kidney) to release free glucose during gluconeogenesis.

Phosphoglucose Isomerase
Glucose-6-phosphate → Fructose-6-phosphate
Reversible
▼
Substrate
Glucose-6-phosphate
Product
Fructose-6-phosphate
Reaction Type
Isomerisation (aldose → ketose)
Explanation
It's worth pausing on why this step exists at all, since nothing is added, removed, or charged — it's a pure rearrangement. The answer lies in step 4. Aldolase, the enzyme that cleaves the sugar in two, needs a ketose to do its job — its mechanism depends on a ketone group at C2 to stabilise the cleavage. Glucose-6-phosphate is an aldose (carbonyl at C1), so before the molecule can be split, it has to be converted to the ketose form. That conversion is the entire purpose of this step.

Because no bonds are made or broken that release or require significant energy, the reaction sits close to equilibrium — it can run either direction depending on which way the concentrations push it, which is exactly what allows it to also run in reverse during gluconeogenesis without needing a separate bypass enzyme.

Inter-pathway link: Pentose Phosphate PathwayF6P can re-enter the non-oxidative branch of the Pentose Phosphate Pathway, interconnecting carbohydrate metabolism.
3
Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1)
Fructose-6-phosphate → Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate
Rate-Limiting Irreversible Regulated
▼
⭐
THE primary rate-limiting and committed step of glycolysis. PFK-1 is the most important regulatory enzyme in the pathway. The cell's energy status is essentially read here — high energy = slow down, low energy = speed up.

Phosphorylation (committed)

⊖ ATP (high concentrations — allosteric) ⊖ Citrate (signals TCA cycle saturation) ⊖ H⁺ (low pH — anaerobic conditions)
Activators
⊕ AMP / ADP (signals low energy) ⊕ Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F-2,6-BP) — most potent activator ⊕ Inorganic phosphate (Pᵢ)
Explanation
PFK-1 is an allosteric enzyme with both an active site and multiple regulatory sites. ATP plays a dual role — it is both a substrate and an allosteric inhibitor. At high [ATP], ATP binds to an allosteric site (not the active site) and reduces the enzyme's affinity for fructose-6-phosphate, slowing glycolysis when energy is abundant.

Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F-2,6-BP) is produced by Phosphofructokinase-2 (PFK-2), a bifunctional enzyme regulated hormonally. Insulin promotes F-2,6-BP production → activates PFK-1 → speeds up glycolysis. Glucagon promotes F-2,6-BP degradation → inhibits PFK-1 → slows glycolysis.

Citrate inhibition makes physiological sense: if the TCA cycle is already saturated with substrate, there is no need to push more pyruvate through glycolysis.

Inter-pathway link: GluconeogenesisIn gluconeogenesis, Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase bypasses this irreversible step, converting F-1,6-BP back to F6P. PFK-1 and F-1,6-bisphosphatase are reciprocally regulated to prevent futile cycling.
4
Aldolase
Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate → DHAP + Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate
Reversible
▼
Substrate
Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate
Products
Dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) + Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P)
Reaction Type
Aldol cleavage (C–C bond cleavage)
Explanation
This is the moment the 6-carbon backbone is finally cut. Aldolase cleaves Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate between C3 and C4, producing two different 3-carbon fragments — Dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P). They're not interchangeable products of equal status: G3P can feed directly into step 6, but DHAP cannot. Without step 5 converting DHAP into G3P, half of every glucose molecule would be stranded.

Worth noticing thermodynamically: this reaction has a strongly positive ΔG°' — it's energetically unfavourable on its own. It only proceeds because the cell continuously removes G3P downstream (Le Chatelier's principle in action), pulling the equilibrium forward. This is a recurring theme in glycolysis — several "uphill" steps run forward only because the next step keeps draining the product away.

Inter-pathway link: Glycerol SynthesisDHAP can be reduced to glycerol-3-phosphate, which is the backbone of triacylglycerols and phospholipids — linking glycolysis to lipid synthesis.
5
Triose Phosphate Isomerase
DHAP → Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate
Reversible
▼
Substrate
Dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP)
Product
Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P)
Reaction Type
Isomerisation
Explanation
Without this step, half of glucose's carbon and energy potential would be wasted as a metabolic dead end — DHAP cannot enter step 6 directly, so converting it to G3P is what rescues the second half of the molecule. This is the doubling point of glycolysis: from here onward, every reaction processes two molecules of G3P for every one molecule of glucose that entered at step 1. That's the entire reason the ATP and NADH yields of steps 6–10 are multiplied by two in the final accounting — it isn't a separate rule, it's a direct consequence of this conversion.

Triose Phosphate Isomerase is often cited as a textbook example of catalytic perfection — its reaction rate is limited only by how fast substrate molecules can diffuse to it, not by the chemistry itself. That matters here because DHAP, left unconverted, can spontaneously degrade into the cytotoxic compound methylglyoxal — so the speed of this enzyme also functions as damage control.
6
Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH)
G3P + NAD⁺ + Pᵢ → 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate + NADH
Reversible NADH produced
▼
Substrate
Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate + Pᵢ
Product
1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (1,3-BPG)
Coenzyme
NAD⁺ → NADH + H⁺ (electron carrier)
Reaction Type
Oxidation + substrate-level phosphorylation
Inhibitors
⊖ NADH (product inhibition — when NAD⁺ is depleted) ⊖ Iodoacetate (irreversible inhibitor — used in research)
Explanation
This is the only oxidation step in glycolysis. G3P is oxidised and simultaneously phosphorylated (using inorganic phosphate, not ATP) to form the high-energy compound 1,3-BPG. NAD⁺ is the electron acceptor, producing NADH.

NAD⁺ availability is critical. If NADH cannot be reoxidised back to NAD⁺ (which occurs in the mitochondria via the electron transport chain), glycolysis stalls. This is why anaerobic conditions drive fermentation — lactate dehydrogenase reduces pyruvate to lactate while reoxidising NADH to NAD⁺, keeping glycolysis running.

Yield reminder: ×2 per glucose → produces 2 NADH total

This recovers the 2 ATP invested in steps 1 and 3. The cell is now at break-even.

Inter-pathway link: 2,3-BPG in RBCsIn red blood cells, 1,3-BPG can be converted to 2,3-BPG by bisphosphoglycerate mutase. 2,3-BPG is a critical regulator of haemoglobin's oxygen affinity, reducing it to facilitate O₂ release in tissues.
8
Phosphoglycerate Mutase
3-Phosphoglycerate → 2-Phosphoglycerate
Reversible
▼
Substrate
3-Phosphoglycerate
Product
2-Phosphoglycerate
Mechanistic Intermediate
2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) — primes the enzyme active site
Reaction Type
Mutase (intramolecular phosphate transfer)
Explanation
At first glance this looks like a pointless shuffle — the phosphate just moves from C3 to C2, same molecule, same phosphate count. But the position matters enormously for what comes next. Step 9 needs to generate a very high-energy phosphate bond by removing water, and that dehydration chemistry only works efficiently when the phosphate sits at C2, adjacent to the carbon that will form the double bond. With the phosphate still at C3, the energetics of step 9 wouldn't line up.

Mechanistically, this isn't a simple intramolecular jump — it goes through a 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate intermediate, with the enzyme itself needing to be primed by a phosphate group (donated initially by 2,3-BPG) before it can catalyse the shift. This is the same 2,3-BPG that, in red blood cells, separately functions as the allosteric regulator of haemoglobin's oxygen affinity — a different role for a molecule that also passes through here as a mechanistic intermediate.
9
Enolase
2-Phosphoglycerate → Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) + H₂O
Reversible
▼
Substrate
2-Phosphoglycerate
Product
Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)
Inorganic Cofactor
Mg²⁺ (or Mn²⁺)
Reaction Type
Dehydration (elimination of H₂O)
Inhibitors
⊖ Fluoride ions (F⁻) — forms fluorophosphate complex with Mg²⁺
Explanation
It's worth understanding why removing a water molecule creates so much energetic potential, since that's not obvious on the surface. 2-Phosphoglycerate is a fairly unremarkable low-energy phosphate ester. Dehydration converts it into an enol phosphate — and enol phosphates are inherently unstable, because the more thermodynamically favoured form is the keto tautomer. The molecule is, in effect, primed to snap into a lower-energy shape the instant the phosphate leaves — and that tautomerisation, released in step 10, is what drives ATP synthesis. The energy isn't stored in the phosphate bond itself so much as in the tension of holding the molecule in its less stable enol form.

Fluoride inhibition is the clinically relevant takeaway: fluoride complexes with Mg²⁺ and phosphate to block Enolase's active site. In toothpaste, this targets oral bacteria — without functional Enolase, they cannot complete glycolysis or produce the organic acids that demineralise enamel, which is the actual mechanism behind fluoride's anti-caries effect.

Glucagon → cAMP → PKA → phosphorylates Pyruvate Kinase → inhibits it in liver. This prevents pyruvate production during fasting, diverting PEP into gluconeogenesis instead.

GluconeogenesisIn gluconeogenesis, this irreversible step is bypassed by two enzymes: Pyruvate Carboxylase (pyruvate → OAA) and PEPCK (OAA → PEP). Alanine CyclePyruvate can be transaminated to alanine in muscle and transported to the liver, where it is reconverted to pyruvate for gluconeogenesis — the glucose-alanine cycle.
Regulatory Overview
Notice what steps 1, 3, and 10 have in common: they are the three irreversible steps. That's not a coincidence — it's the entire logic of where regulation sits in any pathway. A reversible step can't be a meaningful control point, because the cell could just push mass-action equilibrium back and forth without committing to anything. Only an irreversible step represents a genuine decision the cell has made. So the question "which steps are regulated?" and "which steps are irreversible?" have, in glycolysis, the exact same answer.

Of these three, PFK-1 carries by far the most regulatory weight, because it sits at the pathway's true point of no return — by step 3, the cell has already spent its first ATP at step 1, and F6P can still be diverted back toward glycogen or the pentose phosphate pathway. Once PFK-1 acts, the molecule is committed to completing glycolysis. That makes it the natural place to concentrate the cell's energy-sensing machinery.

Reading the signals together: PFK-1 effectively integrates three separate energy-status readouts into one decision. The ATP:AMP ratio gives a direct, immediate readout of the cell's own energy charge. Citrate gives a readout one pathway downstream — it tells PFK-1 whether the Krebs cycle still has capacity to receive more carbon, which prevents glycolysis from overproducing pyruvate that has nowhere useful to go. And F-2,6-BP gives a readout from outside the cell entirely — the hormonal state of the whole body, fed or fasting — overriding the local signals when the bigger picture demands it (insulin pushes glycolysis forward even if local ATP is adequate, because the liver needs to process incoming glucose). No single signal could do this job alone; it's the combination that makes PFK-1 such an effective control point.

Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: Pyruvate Kinase (Step 10)
Autosomal RecessiveHaemolytic Anaemia

Start from what the red blood cell actually has to work with. It has no mitochondria, so the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain are not options — glycolysis is the cell's only ATP-generating pathway, and steps 7 and 10 are its only two ATP-producing reactions. Knock out step 10, and the RBC loses half of its total ATP yield outright, with no backup system to compensate.

Trace the consequence forward. ATP powers the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump that keeps the RBC's ionic gradient — and therefore its shape and membrane integrity — intact. Without enough ATP, the pump fails, the cell can't regulate its internal ion balance, and the membrane becomes rigid and fragile. The spleen recognises these abnormal cells and removes them prematurely: this is chronic haemolytic anaemia.

Upstream of the block, substrate backs up — phosphoenolpyruvate and the intermediates before it accumulate. One redirection is worth knowing well: 1,3-BPG, unable to proceed efficiently down the blocked pathway, is shunted instead into 2,3-BPG via the Rapoport-Luebering shunt. 2,3-BPG rises as a result — and because 2,3-BPG binds deoxyhaemoglobin and lowers its oxygen affinity, this becomes a partial physiological compensation: the surviving RBCs release oxygen to tissues more readily, even though there are fewer of them.
Lactic Acidosis
Mechanism: NAD⁺ depletion forces pyruvate toward lactate
AcquiredEmergency Presentation

Step 6 (GAPDH) is the only oxidative step in glycolysis, and it has one strict requirement: a continuous supply of NAD⁺. Under normal aerobic conditions, the NADH produced there is reoxidised back to NAD⁺ in the mitochondria via the electron transport chain — but that route depends on oxygen as the final electron acceptor.

Cut off oxygen — through shock, severe anaemia, or any state of poor tissue perfusion — and the ETC stalls. NADH can no longer be reoxidised, NAD⁺ runs low, and GAPDH grinds to a halt without it. Glycolysis would stop entirely here if not for one escape route: Lactate Dehydrogenase reduces the accumulating pyruvate to lactate, and in doing so regenerates NAD⁺ from NADH. This keeps step 6 supplied and glycolysis running anaerobically, but at a cost — lactate accumulates in the blood, and being an acid, it lowers blood pH, producing metabolic (lactic) acidosis.

Two non-hypoxic causes are worth knowing for the same reason: metformin impairs hepatic lactate clearance and mitochondrial function, pushing the same NADH/NAD⁺ imbalance; and thiamine (Vitamin B₁) deficiency cripples Pyruvate Dehydrogenase (which requires TPP, a thiamine-derived cofactor, to convert pyruvate to Acetyl-CoA) — pyruvate has nowhere to go but back to lactate.
Phosphoglucose Isomerase Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: Phosphoglucose Isomerase (Step 2)
Autosomal RecessiveRare

The second most common glycolytic enzymopathy after Pyruvate Kinase deficiency, and the same underlying logic applies: RBCs cannot tolerate any interruption to glycolytic ATP production, so a block at step 2 produces the same end result — chronic non-spherocytic haemolytic anaemia — even though the enzyme involved is completely different. Glucose-6-phosphate accumulates proximal to the block. A useful exam distinction: unlike Pyruvate Kinase deficiency, some cases present with additional neurological features, because the enzyme also has functions outside red blood cells.
