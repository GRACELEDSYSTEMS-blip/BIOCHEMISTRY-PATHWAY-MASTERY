Why Does the ETC Exist? The Big Picture

Glycolysis produces 2 ATP per glucose. The Krebs cycle produces 2 more. That's 4 ATP from all the enzymatic reactions you've studied so far. But cells need enormous amounts of ATP — a resting human uses roughly 40 kg of ATP per day. 4 ATP from one glucose is nowhere near enough.

The key insight: glycolysis and the Krebs cycle don't just make ATP directly — they also make NADH and FADH₂. These are electron carriers that have captured high-energy electrons from the oxidation of glucose carbons. Those electrons contain potential energy. The ETC's job is to extract that potential energy in a controlled way and use it to make ATP.

Think of it this way: NADH is like a charged battery. The ETC is the circuit that allows the battery to discharge in a controlled, useful manner — instead of releasing all the energy as heat at once, the ETC releases it stepwise, using each step to do work (pumping protons). The proton gradient is then used by ATP Synthase like a turbine uses flowing water — to generate rotational mechanical energy that drives ATP synthesis.

🌊 The Dam and Turbine Analogy
NADH/FADH₂ = water held at height behind a dam (stored potential energy)
The ETC complexes = the controlled channels in the dam — electrons flow through them "downhill" to oxygen, releasing energy at each step
Proton pumping = water flowing through a channel, driving a turbine (the proton gradient is the turbine position — potential energy now stored as an ion gradient)
ATP Synthase = the turbine itself — protons flowing back down the gradient spin its rotor, generating ATP
Oxygen = the drain at the bottom — accepts the electrons at the end and combines with protons to form water

The key concept: the energy is NOT released all at once — it's controlled and stepwise, which is what makes it so efficient. A fire releases all the energy in glucose as heat at once. Cellular respiration extracts most of it as useful ATP.
🔑
The First Law of Thermodynamics applies here: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed. The chemical energy in C–H bonds of glucose is transformed → into chemical energy of NADH/FADH₂ → into electrical energy of the proton gradient → into mechanical energy of ATP synthase rotation → into chemical energy of the ATP phosphoanhydride bond. Each transformation loses some energy as heat — this is why the process is not 100% efficient.
Mitochondrial Structure — Form Fits Function

The mitochondrion is a double-membraned organelle. Its structure is perfectly engineered for its function — every compartment has a specific role in energy metabolism. Understanding the structure explains why the ETC works the way it does.

🔵 Outer Membrane
Freely permeable to small molecules and ions via protein channels called porins (limit ~10,000 Daltons). Contains enzymes for phospholipid synthesis, fatty acid desaturation, and elongation. Not a major barrier — the outer membrane is relatively leaky.
🟠 Inner Membrane
Highly impermeable — the most protein-rich biological membrane known (4:1 protein:lipid ratio). Contains the entire ETC (Complexes I–IV), ATP Synthase, and specific metabolite transporters. Impermeability is essential — the proton gradient can only be maintained if protons cannot leak through the membrane except via ATP Synthase. Highly folded into cristae to maximise surface area for ETC complexes and ATP Synthase.
🟢 Matrix
Protein-rich (~500 mg/mL — almost gel-like). Contains: TCA cycle enzymes, Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Complex, beta-oxidation enzymes, amino acid oxidation enzymes, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), ribosomes for translating mtDNA-encoded proteins. The matrix is where NADH and FADH₂ are produced — right next to where they are used by the ETC.
🟣 Intermembrane Space (IMS)
The space between the outer and inner membranes. Composition similar to cytosol (because the outer membrane is freely permeable). This is where protons (H⁺) accumulate as the ETC pumps them out of the matrix — creating the proton concentration gradient. Also contains Cytochrome c (a mobile electron carrier).
💡
Why are cristae so important? The highly folded inner membrane dramatically increases the surface area available for embedding ETC complexes and ATP Synthase. Muscle cells (which have very high ATP demand) have mitochondria with many densely packed cristae. Liver cells have fewer, less dense cristae — their ATP demand per mitochondrion is lower. This explains why muscle mitochondria produce more ATP per unit volume.
Thermodynamics — Why Electrons Flow "Downhill"

The ETC works because electrons spontaneously flow from carriers with more negative reduction potentials to carriers with more positive reduction potentials. This is exactly like water flowing downhill — it's thermodynamically spontaneous because it moves to lower potential energy.

Reduction potential (E°′) measures the tendency of a molecule to gain electrons. The more positive the E°′, the stronger the molecule's tendency to accept electrons (gain electrons = be reduced).

Key values from the slides:
NADH: E°′ = −0.32 V — strongly negative, meaning NADH readily gives up its electrons (it's a strong reducing agent)
O₂: E°′ = +0.82 V — strongly positive, meaning O₂ readily accepts electrons (it's a strong oxidising agent)

The difference: ΔE°′ = 0.82 − (−0.32) = +1.14 V. A positive ΔE°′ means the reaction is spontaneous. The electrons fall from NADH (−0.32 V) down through the ETC complexes in stepwise fashion, each step releasing some energy, until they reach O₂ (+0.82 V) — the terminal electron acceptor.

Plain English
Think of the reduction potentials as a staircase. NADH is at the top (−0.32 V, high energy, wants to give electrons). O₂ is at the bottom (+0.82 V, low energy, wants to accept electrons). The four complexes are the steps in between — each one is at a slightly higher reduction potential than the previous one. Electrons fall down the stairs step by step, releasing energy at each step. The formula ΔG°′ = −nFΔE°′ converts this electrical potential difference into the free energy available for ATP synthesis.
Substrate-Level vs Oxidative Phosphorylation — The Key Distinction

Substrate-level phosphorylation: ATP is made directly when a phosphate group is transferred from a high-energy substrate molecule to ADP. No proton gradient required. Examples: Phosphoglycerate Kinase and Pyruvate Kinase in glycolysis; Succinyl-CoA Synthetase in the TCA cycle. Can occur in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. Produces only a small amount of ATP.

Oxidative phosphorylation: ATP is made indirectly — the energy from electron transfer through the ETC is first stored as a proton gradient, and that gradient drives ATP Synthase. Requires O₂ as the final electron acceptor. Produces the vast majority of ATP (28 out of ~30–32 total). This is why inhibiting the ETC or removing O₂ is so catastrophic — it shuts down the source of ~90% of the cell's ATP.

The Critical Difference
Substrate-level phosphorylation is like being paid in cash at each step of work. Oxidative phosphorylation is like putting all your earnings in a bank (the proton gradient) and then cashing out in bulk via ATP Synthase. The bank system is far more efficient and produces far more total ATP — but it requires the infrastructure (the ETC, the proton-impermeable inner membrane, and O₂).

The Two Mobile Electron Carriers — CoQ and Cytochrome c

The four complexes are large, fixed protein assemblies embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electrons cannot jump directly from one complex to the next — they need mobile carriers to ferry electrons between complexes. There are exactly two: Coenzyme Q (Ubiquinone) and Cytochrome c.

🔶
Coenzyme Q (Ubiquinone / CoQ)
Also: UQ, Ubiquinone, CoQ10
A lipid-soluble small molecule that can diffuse freely within the hydrophobic core of the inner mitochondrial membrane. It collects electrons from both Complex I (from NADH) and Complex II (from FADH₂) and delivers them to Complex III. Because it is lipid-soluble, it can move laterally within the membrane. It shuttles between oxidised (ubiquinone, Q) and reduced (ubiquinol, QH₂) forms.

Key: CoQ accepts 2 electrons at a time (from FADH₂ or via FMN in Complex I) but passes them one at a time to Complex III via the Q cycle.
🔷
Cytochrome c (Cyt c)
Water-soluble · Located in IMS
A small water-soluble protein located in the intermembrane space. It carries electrons from Complex III to Complex IV — one electron at a time (it contains a single heme iron that alternates between Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺). Because it is water-soluble, it diffuses freely through the aqueous IMS.

Key: Cytochrome c carries only ONE electron at a time. This is why Complex III must run a special mechanism (the Q cycle) to convert the 2-electron transfer from CoQ into sequential 1-electron transfers to cytochrome c.

Cytochrome c is also released from the IMS into the cytosol during apoptosis (programmed cell death) — it activates caspases and triggers the death cascade. Apoptosis LinkCytochrome c release from the IMS is a trigger for the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. When the mitochondrial membrane is permeabilised (by pro-apoptotic signals), Cyt c floods into the cytosol and binds Apaf-1, forming the apoptosome, which activates Caspase-9 and the caspase cascade.

The mechanism step by step:
1. NADH donates 2 electrons and a proton (H⁺) to FMN (Flavin Mononucleotide) — the prosthetic group of Complex I. NADH → NAD⁺ + H⁺ + 2e⁻; FMN → FMNH₂.

2. FMNH₂ then passes electrons one at a time through a series of iron-sulfur (Fe-S) clusters — protein-bound clusters of iron and sulfur atoms that alternate between Fe²⁺ (reduced) and Fe³⁺ (oxidised) states, each passing one electron at a time. 3. The electrons eventually reduce Coenzyme Q (ubiquinone → ubiquinol, QH₂).

4. Simultaneously, the energy from this electron transfer is used to pump 4 protons from the matrix to the IMS. The mechanism involves conformational changes in the protein complex driven by the electron transfer.

The Q Cycle simplified:
QH₂ (ubiquinol) from Complex I or II binds to Complex III. One of its two electrons goes to Cyt c (via the Rieske Fe-S cluster and Cyt c₁) — this is the "useful" electron going forward. The other electron goes backwards through Cyt bH and Cyt bL to partially re-reduce another CoQ molecule. On the second pass, the same thing happens — another electron goes to Cyt c, and the partially reduced CoQ gets fully reduced and picks up 2 protons from the matrix to regenerate QH₂.

The result: for every 2 electrons passing through Complex III to Cytochrome c, 4 protons are pumped into the IMS (2 released from QH₂ at the outer face + 2 consumed from the matrix to re-reduce CoQ). The Q cycle amplifies proton pumping.

Inhibited by: Antimycin A (blocks the electron transfer between Cyt bH and CoQ), Dimercaprol (also called BAL — British Anti-Lewisite), TTFA.

Why copper centres? The reduction of O₂ to water requires a 4-electron transfer (O₂ + 4e⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2H₂O). But Cytochrome c only delivers one electron at a time. Complex IV must accumulate 4 electrons before reducing one O₂ molecule. The two copper centres (CuA and CuB) and two cytochrome a centres (Cyt a and Cyt a₃) act as a holding reservoir — electrons from Cyt c pass through CuA → Cyt a → Cyt a₃/CuB, which is where the actual O₂ binding and reduction occurs.

Why is this important? Partially reduced oxygen intermediates (superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical) are extremely reactive and damaging. By accumulating 4 electrons before reducing O₂, Complex IV ensures O₂ is reduced all the way to water in a controlled manner, minimising the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS).

Inhibited by: Cyanide (CN⁻), Carbon monoxide (CO), Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), Azide (N₃⁻) — all bind to the iron in Cyt a₃ or the copper in CuB, blocking O₂ binding and reducing. This is why these are all deadly poisons — they shut down the entire ETC by blocking the terminal step.

Mitchell's Chemiosmotic Theory — The Founding Idea

Before 1961, no one knew how the ETC was coupled to ATP synthesis. The prevailing hypothesis was that there must be a high-energy chemical intermediate — a mysterious "~X" that captured energy from electron transport and used it to make ATP. Nobody could find it.

Peter Mitchell proposed a radically different idea in 1961: the coupling is not chemical but osmotic. The energy of electron transport is stored as an electrochemical proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane — not in a chemical intermediate. This gradient is then used to drive ATP synthesis. Mitchell called this chemiosmosis, and won the Nobel Prize for it in 1978.

The chemiosmotic theory states: coupling of electron transfer to ATP synthesis is indirect, via a H⁺ electrochemical gradient. There are three components:

1
Respiration creates the gradient
Spontaneous electron flow through Complexes I, III, and IV is coupled to non-spontaneous H⁺ ejection from the matrix. The matrix becomes alkaline and negatively charged relative to the IMS (which becomes acidic and positively charged). This creates two components of the proton motive force: a chemical gradient (ΔpH) and an electrical gradient (Δψ, membrane potential). Together they form the proton motive force (PMF).
2
ATP Synthase uses the gradient
Non-spontaneous ATP synthesis is coupled to spontaneous H⁺ transport back into the matrix via ATP Synthase. The pH and electrical gradients created by respiration are together the driving force for H⁺ uptake through the Fₒ subunit of ATP Synthase. Return of protons to the matrix "uses up" the gradient — and each proton returning drives a conformational change in the F₁ subunit that synthesises ATP.
3
ATP/ADP exchange across the membrane
ATP produced in the matrix must exit to the cytosol where it is used. ADP and Pᵢ from cytosolic ATP hydrolysis must re-enter the matrix. This exchange is carried out by the ADP/ATP Translocase (adenine nucleotide translocase) — an antiporter in the inner membrane that exchanges ATP (out) for ADP (in). Pᵢ enters via a separate phosphate carrier. Atractyloside inhibits this translocase, trapping ADP in the cytosol and blocking all oxidative phosphorylation.
The Proton Motive Force — Two Gradients in One

The proton motive force (PMF) is the total driving force for proton re-entry into the matrix through ATP Synthase. It has two components that both pull protons back into the matrix:

Chemical gradient (ΔpH): The IMS has a higher concentration of H⁺ (lower pH) than the matrix. Protons naturally flow down this concentration gradient from IMS to matrix — this is diffusion.

Electrical gradient (Δψ, membrane potential): The IMS is positively charged (excess H⁺) and the matrix is negatively charged. Protons (which are positively charged) are attracted toward the negative matrix — this is an electrostatic force.

Both components act in the same direction — both drive protons from IMS back into the matrix. Together they make the PMF a very powerful driving force. The PMF is analogous to the voltage across a battery — it represents stored electrical potential energy that can be converted into work (ATP synthesis) when protons are allowed to flow through ATP Synthase.

🔋 The Battery Analogy
The proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane IS the battery. The ETC charges the battery (pumps protons against their gradient). ATP Synthase is the device that uses the battery (allows protons to flow back down, converting the electrical potential energy to mechanical → chemical energy). If you remove ATP Synthase (e.g. with oligomycin), the battery stays charged but no work is done — the gradient builds up but no ATP is made. If you add an uncoupler (e.g. DNP), you short-circuit the battery — protons leak back through the membrane directly, energy is lost as heat, and no ATP is made even though the ETC continues running.
ATP Synthase (Complex V) — The Molecular Power Generator

ATP Synthase is one of the most remarkable molecular machines in biology — a nanoscale rotary motor that directly converts the flow of protons into the mechanical rotation of a protein subunit, which then drives the chemical synthesis of ATP. It is embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane and is the only pathway for proton re-entry into the matrix (when the ETC is operating normally).

ATP Synthase has two major domains:

Fₒ subunit (the rotor, embedded in the membrane): A ring of c subunits embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Protons from the IMS bind to the c ring, causing it to rotate. The more protons flow through, the faster it spins. The c ring is connected to a central stalk that passes into the F₁ domain.

F₁ subunit (the catalytic head, in the matrix): A hexamer of alternating α and β subunits arranged around the central stalk (γ subunit). The β subunits contain the catalytic sites where ATP is synthesised. As the γ stalk rotates (driven by the Fₒ rotation below), it causes conformational changes in the β subunits in a sequential, three-phase cycle — each β subunit cycles through: empty → ADP+Pᵢ bound → ATP synthesised → ATP released. This is Paul Boyer's binding-change mechanism (Nobel Prize 1997).

Approximately 3–4 protons must pass through ATP Synthase to synthesise one ATP molecule. Given that NADH generates 10 protons and FADH₂ generates 6 protons, this gives the new ATP yields of 2.5 per NADH and 1.5 per FADH₂.

⚙️
ATP Synthase inhibited by Oligomycin — Oligomycin binds to the Fₒ subunit and physically blocks the proton channel. Protons cannot flow back into the matrix → PMF builds up → the ETC slows and stops (because the gradient becomes so large that pumping more protons is thermodynamically impossible — the ETC is "backed up" by its own product). This is why ATP synthase inhibitors also inhibit the ETC — the two are tightly coupled. DCCD (Dicyclohexylcarbodiimide) also blocks the Fₒ proton channel.
Shuttling Cytoplasmic NADH into the Mitochondria

NADH produced in the cytoplasm (by glycolysis at the Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase step) cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane — it is too large and the membrane is impermeable to it. Its electrons must be "shuttled" in using one of two systems. The shuttle chosen determines whether the cytoplasmic NADH effectively becomes mitochondrial NADH (efficient) or mitochondrial FADH₂ (less efficient).

✅ Malate-Aspartate Shuttle (Efficient)
Used primarily in liver, heart, and kidney.

How it works:
1. Cytoplasmic NADH reduces OAA → Malate (Malate Dehydrogenase)
2. Malate enters the matrix via the malate-α-ketoglutarate antiporter
3. Inside the matrix, Malate is oxidised back to OAA → generating mitochondrial NADH (NAD⁺ + H⁺)
4. OAA is transaminated to Aspartate → exits the matrix
5. Aspartate is converted back to OAA in the cytoplasm to continue the cycle

Result: Each cytoplasmic NADH generates 1 mitochondrial NADH → 2.5 ATP (new values). This shuttle doesn't bypass Complex I, so the full 10 protons are pumped. Efficient.
⚠️ Glycerol-3-Phosphate Shuttle (Inefficient)
Used primarily in brain and skeletal muscle.

How it works:
1. Cytoplasmic NADH reduces DHAP → Glycerol-3-Phosphate (cytoplasmic G3P Dehydrogenase, uses NAD⁺)
2. Glycerol-3-Phosphate is reoxidised back to DHAP by mitochondrial G3P Dehydrogenase (on the outer face of the inner membrane) — this enzyme uses FAD as cofactor, producing FADH₂
3. FADH₂ passes electrons directly to CoQ (bypassing Complex I)

Result: Each cytoplasmic NADH generates 1 mitochondrial FADH₂ → only 1.5 ATP (new values). This shuttle bypasses Complex I, losing the 4 protons that Complex I would normally pump. Less efficient — but faster and irreversible.
🔑
This shuttle difference affects total ATP yield from glucose. If the malate-aspartate shuttle is used (liver): 2 cytoplasmic NADH × 2.5 ATP = 5 ATP from glycolytic NADH. If glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle (muscle/brain): 2 × 1.5 ATP = 3 ATP. This is why the total ATP yield from glucose varies depending on tissue — it's not a fixed number.

Role of Oxygen — Why We Breathe

Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor of the ETC. At Complex IV, electrons combine with O₂ and H⁺ to form water. Without O₂:

→ Complex IV cannot accept electrons → electron flow through the entire ETC backs up → no protons are pumped → no PMF → no ATP from oxidative phosphorylation → cells run out of energy → cell death

This is why oxygen deprivation is lethal within minutes. Cells that are most dependent on oxidative phosphorylation (neurons, cardiomyocytes) die the fastest — neurons can tolerate only about 4–6 minutes of complete oxygen deprivation before irreversible damage begins.

In the absence of O₂, cells can generate ATP only via substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis and the TCA cycle — but the TCA cycle itself requires NAD⁺ regeneration via the ETC, so without O₂, the TCA cycle also stops. Only glycolysis remains, generating 2 ATP per glucose — woefully insufficient for sustained cell function.

⚠️ Inhibitors of oxidative phosphorylation include some of the most lethal poisons known to medicine. Understanding their mechanism explains both their toxicity and (in some cases) their therapeutic use.
Two Classes — Inhibitors vs Uncouplers

Respiration Inhibitors block a specific step in the ETC or ATP synthesis. They prevent electron flow or proton pumping. The result: the ETC stops, the proton gradient cannot be built, and ATP synthesis ceases. Both ETC activity and ATP synthesis are inhibited simultaneously.

Uncouplers do NOT block electron flow — they allow the ETC to continue running normally. Instead, they dissipate the proton gradient by providing an alternative pathway for protons to re-enter the matrix (bypassing ATP Synthase). The result: ETC runs, O₂ is consumed, NADH is oxidised — but no ATP is made. The energy that would have driven ATP synthesis is released as heat.

The distinction is important: with an inhibitor, you can tell if ATP Synthase coupling is intact by adding an uncoupler (if the ETC then runs, coupling was intact but the ETC was blocked). With an uncoupler, the ETC keeps running but ATP isn't made.

Uncouplers — Breaking the Link Between ETC and ATP

Uncouplers are hydrophobic molecules with a dissociable proton. They can pick up a proton on one side of the inner mitochondrial membrane, diffuse through the hydrophobic membrane (because they are lipid-soluble), and release the proton on the other side. This effectively provides a "leak" pathway for protons — they flow back into the matrix without going through ATP Synthase. The proton gradient is continuously dissipated as the ETC pumps protons out and uncouplers bring them straight back in. Energy is released as heat.

🌡️
Why uncouplers are dangerous as diet drugs: They work — fat is burned as heat instead of being stored. But the body cannot regulate the uncoupling. If you take too much, the ETC runs at maximum speed burning all available fuel as heat, core body temperature rises uncontrollably (malignant hyperthermia), and the cell has no ATP for any function. Death occurs from a combination of hyperthermia, cardiovascular collapse from lack of cardiac ATP, and seizures. The therapeutic window is essentially zero.
Inhibitors vs Uncouplers — The Key Distinction for Exams
Inhibitor (e.g. cyanide, rotenone): The ETC STOPS. No O₂ consumed. No protons pumped. No PMF. No ATP. If you add an uncoupler now — still nothing happens (the chain is blocked upstream).

Uncoupler (e.g. DNP): The ETC runs FASTER. More O₂ consumed. More NADH oxidised. But protons leak back without making ATP. No PMF maintained. No ATP. If you add oligomycin (ATP synthase inhibitor) — the ETC slows because the PMF can no longer be dissipated via ATP Synthase, so it builds up and back-pressure stops pumping.
Cyanide and Carbon Monoxide Poisoning
Complex IV inhibitors — block terminal electron acceptance
Medical EmergencyMinutes to death without treatment

Cyanide (CN⁻): Binds to Fe³⁺ in Cytochrome a₃ of Complex IV with extremely high affinity, preventing O₂ binding. The entire ETC backs up — no protons pumped, no ATP made. Cells cannot use O₂ even though it is present — a state sometimes called "histotoxic hypoxia" (the tissue is hypoxic at the cellular level despite normal blood O₂). Cells switch to anaerobic glycolysis → massive lactic acidosis. Clinical features: rapid unconsciousness, seizures, cardiovascular collapse, cherry-red skin (venous blood is well-oxygenated because tissues can't extract O₂). Treatment: Hydroxocobalamin (binds cyanide → cyanocobalamin, excreted); Sodium thiosulfate (converts CN⁻ → thiocyanate via rhodanase); Nitrites (generate methaemoglobin which competes with Complex IV for cyanide binding — Dicobalt EDTA is an alternative).

Carbon Monoxide (CO): Also inhibits Complex IV (binds CuB) AND binds haemoglobin (forming carboxyhaemoglobin, which cannot carry O₂). Double toxicity — O₂ cannot be delivered (haemoglobin poisoning) AND cannot be used (ETC poisoning). Treatment: 100% O₂ by mask or hyperbaric O₂ (displaces CO from both haemoglobin and Complex IV by mass action).
Metformin and Lactic Acidosis
Mild Complex I inhibitor — relevant at toxic doses or in renal failure
Type 2 DM treatmentRisk in renal failure

Metformin, the most widely used oral antidiabetic drug, mildly inhibits mitochondrial Complex I. At therapeutic doses, this reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis (by raising the AMP:ATP ratio, activating AMPK) and improves insulin sensitivity — beneficial effects. However, at toxic doses (especially in renal failure where metformin accumulates), the Complex I inhibition becomes significant: reduced ETC activity → less oxidative phosphorylation → cells shift toward anaerobic glycolysis → lactate accumulates → life-threatening lactic acidosis. This is why metformin is contraindicated in significant renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min).
Mitochondrial Diseases — Maternal Inheritance
Mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) affecting ETC components
Non-Mendelian inheritanceMaternal transmissionHeteroplasmy

Mitochondrial diseases are caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) — which encodes 13 proteins (all components of the ETC and ATP Synthase), 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs. Mutations in these genes impair oxidative phosphorylation.

Maternal inheritance: Mitochondria are inherited almost exclusively from the mother — the egg contains ~100,000 mitochondria; the sperm's few mitochondria are actively destroyed after fertilisation. Therefore:
— Only the mother transmits mitochondrial DNA to offspring
— Both males and females can be affected
— All offspring of an affected mother are affected (or at least carry the mutation)
— Affected fathers do NOT transmit the disease to any children

Heteroplasmy: A cell may contain a mix of normal and mutant mitochondria. The proportion of mutant mitochondria determines whether the disease threshold is crossed. This explains variable expressivity — even siblings from the same mother may have different severity.

Why brain and muscle are affected most: Neurons and cardiomyocytes are the most energy-demanding cells in the body — they rely almost entirely on oxidative phosphorylation. Any impairment of the ETC hits them hardest. This is why mitochondrial diseases typically present as neuropathies (peripheral nerve disease) and myopathies (muscle disease), particularly affecting the brain, heart, and skeletal muscle.

Examples of mitochondrial diseases:
— MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, Stroke-like episodes) — mutation in mtDNA tRNA genes
— MERRF (Myoclonic Epilepsy with Ragged Red Fibres) — tRNA Lys mutation
— Leber's Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON) — Complex I subunit mutations → sudden bilateral vision loss in young adults
Statins and Mitochondrial Function
HMG-CoA Reductase inhibitors — multiple mitochondrial effects
Widely prescribedMyopathy risk

Your lecturer's slides specifically mention statins. Statins (HMG-CoA Reductase inhibitors — atorvastatin, simvastatin, etc.) reduce cholesterol synthesis by blocking the mevalonate pathway. However, the same mevalonate pathway produces Coenzyme Q10 (ubiquinone) — a critical ETC component. Statins therefore reduce CoQ10 levels in mitochondria, impairing electron transfer from Complexes I and II to Complex III.

Additional mitochondrial effects from the slides: inhibition of respiratory chain complexes directly, induction of mitochondrial apoptosis, dysregulation of Ca²⁺ metabolism, reduced CPT-2 expression (impairs fatty acid transport).

Clinical consequence: Statin-induced myopathy — muscle pain, weakness, and in severe cases rhabdomyolysis — is partly explained by mitochondrial dysfunction in muscle cells (which are highly dependent on oxidative phosphorylation). CoQ10 supplementation is sometimes recommended for patients with statin-induced myopathy, though evidence is mixed.
Thermogenin (UCP1) and Brown Adipose Tissue
Physiological uncoupling — non-shivering thermogenesis
NewbornsHibernating mammalsObesity research target

Thermogenin (Uncoupling Protein 1 / UCP1) is a physiological uncoupler found in brown adipose tissue (BAT). Unlike white adipose tissue (which stores fat), BAT is packed with mitochondria and is specialised for heat generation.

UCP1 is a proton channel in the inner mitochondrial membrane of brown adipocytes. When activated by free fatty acids (released during cold exposure or sympathetic stimulation), it allows protons to flow back into the matrix without going through ATP Synthase — uncoupling the ETC from ATP synthesis and converting the proton gradient energy directly into heat. This non-shivering thermogenesis is critically important for:
— Newborns (who cannot shiver effectively and have a high body surface area:volume ratio — they lose heat rapidly)
— Hibernating mammals (generating heat during arousal from hibernation)

Adult humans have small amounts of metabolically active BAT (detectable by PET scanning), and UCP1 is a major research target for anti-obesity therapy — increasing BAT activity could increase caloric expenditure without exercise.
Assignment Questions from Your Lecturer

Mechanistic questions to think through:

1. "What is the mechanistic basis for the observation that inhibitors of ATP synthase also lead to inhibition of the electron transport chain?" Answer: When ATP Synthase is blocked (e.g. by oligomycin), protons cannot re-enter the matrix. The proton gradient (PMF) builds up to its maximum. Eventually, the ETC complexes cannot pump any more protons against this maximal gradient — the "back-pressure" stops electron flow. The ETC is inhibited by its own product (the gradient) when the gradient cannot be relieved by ATP Synthase.

2. "How does inhibition of ADP-ATP translocase affect the citric acid cycle and glycolysis?" Answer: If ADP cannot enter the mitochondria, ADP is not available for ATP Synthase → ATP cannot be made → PMF builds up → ETC stops → NADH cannot be reoxidised → TCA cycle stops (needs NAD⁺). Glycolysis also slows as its ATP product builds up (ATP inhibits PFK-1) and NADH cannot be reoxidised (in aerobic conditions). The entire respiratory machinery backs up from the translocase block.

3. "Why do mitochondria in muscle cells often have more cristae than liver cells?" Answer: Muscle cells (especially cardiac muscle) have far higher ATP demand per unit volume than liver cells. More cristae = more inner membrane surface area = more space to embed ETC complexes and ATP Synthase = higher ATP production capacity per mitochondrion. Form fits function — the number and density of cristae directly reflect the cell's oxidative phosphorylation demand.

Question 4 of 5
Which correctly states a key component of Mitchell's chemiosmotic theory?
Energy of electron transfer is stored in a high-energy chemical intermediate that phosphorylates ADP
Electron transfer through the ETC directly phosphorylates ADP by substrate-level phosphorylation
Energy of electron transfer is stored as an electrochemical proton gradient that drives ATP synthesis when protons flow back through ATP Synthase
ATP synthesis requires direct contact between ETC complexes and ATP Synthase in a supercomplex
Mitchell proposed that coupling is indirect via an electrochemical proton gradient - not via a chemical intermediate. ETC complexes pump protons from matrix to IMS building a PMF (delta pH + delta psi). This PMF drives protons back through the Fo subunit of ATP Synthase causing Fo rotation, which drives conformational changes in F1 that synthesise ATP. No chemical intermediate needed - the gradient IS the intermediate. Nobel Prize 1978.
Question 5 of 5
Which statement about ATP Synthase (Complex V) is correct?
The F1 subunit is embedded in the inner membrane and forms the proton channel
The Fo subunit rotates as protons flow through it, driving conformational changes in F1 that synthesise ATP
Oligomycin inhibits F1 by competing with ADP at the catalytic site
ATP Synthase can only synthesise ATP and cannot hydrolyse it under any circumstances
ATP Synthase: Fo is embedded in the membrane forming the proton channel and rotating as protons flow. F1 is in the matrix with catalytic beta subunits. Fo rotation turns the gamma subunit (central stalk) inside F1, driving sequential conformational changes (binding-change mechanism of Boyer) that synthesise ATP. Oligomycin blocks the Fo proton channel not the F1 catalytic site. ATP Synthase CAN hydrolyse ATP running in reverse - exploited in bacteria.

Assertion
Inhibition of the ADP/ATP translocase by atractyloside also leads to inhibition of the electron transport chain.
Reason
When ADP cannot enter the mitochondria, ATP Synthase has no substrate, causing the proton gradient to build up until back-pressure stops electron flow.
Both correct. Atractyloside blocks the ADP/ATP translocase trapping ADP in the cytosol. Without ADP in the matrix, ATP Synthase cannot synthesise ATP, so protons cannot re-enter through it. The PMF builds to maximum. ETC complexes pump protons against this maximal gradient - when gradient is maximal, pumping becomes thermodynamically impossible. The ETC backs up and stops. This demonstrates tight coupling between ATP Synthase and the ETC.
Assertion
Mitochondrial diseases caused by mtDNA mutations are inherited exclusively through the maternal line.
Reason
Mitochondria in the fertilised egg are derived almost entirely from the egg cell, as paternal mitochondria from sperm are actively degraded after fertilisation.
Both correct. The egg contains approximately 100,000 mitochondria while sperm contributes very few - and those are actively destroyed by ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis after fertilisation. Therefore mtDNA is transmitted essentially only from the mother. Both sons and daughters can be affected, but only daughters pass the mitochondria on. Affected fathers do not transmit mitochondrial disease to any children.
Assertion
Adding an uncoupler to mitochondria treated with oligomycin will cause oxygen consumption to increase.
Reason
Uncouplers dissipate the proton gradient by providing an alternative proton re-entry pathway, relieving the back-pressure on the ETC and allowing electron flow to resume.
Both correct. Oligomycin blocks ATP Synthase causing PMF to build to maximum and stopping the ETC. When an uncoupler (DNP) is added, it dissipates the gradient providing an alternative proton pathway. With the gradient removed, back-pressure on ETC complexes is relieved and electron flow resumes. O2 consumption increases. This is a classic experimental approach distinguishing ETC inhibition from ATP Synthase inhibition.
Assertion
Complex II of the ETC pumps 2 protons per pair of electrons transferred from FADH2 to CoQ.
Reason
Complex II is embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane and contains FAD, iron-sulfur clusters, and cytochrome b560 that facilitate proton translocation.
The assertion is FALSE - Complex II pumps ZERO protons. This is a critical commonly tested fact. Complex II transfers electrons from FADH2 to CoQ without any proton pumping - precisely why FADH2 produces fewer ATP than NADH. The reason contains accurate structural information but the claim about proton translocation is wrong. Both assertion and reason are false = D.
Assertion
The malate-aspartate shuttle is more efficient than the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle for transferring cytoplasmic NADH.
Reason
The malate-aspartate shuttle produces mitochondrial NADH yielding 2.5 ATP per cytoplasmic NADH, whereas the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle produces mitochondrial FADH2 yielding only 1.5 ATP per cytoplasmic NADH.
Both correct. Malate-aspartate shuttle: cytoplasmic NADH reduces OAA to malate, malate enters matrix and is reoxidised generating mitochondrial NADH = 2.5 ATP. Glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle: cytoplasmic NADH reduces DHAP to G3P, mitochondrial G3P dehydrogenase (FAD-linked) reoxidises it producing FADH2 = 1.5 ATP. The shuttle chosen affects total ATP yield from glucose and explains why yield varies between tissues.
