The gateway to the Krebs cycle. PDC converts pyruvate (3C) irreversibly into Acetyl-CoA (2C), releasing one CO₂. It is a multi-enzyme complex — one of the largest known — and requires five distinct cofactors. Committing pyruvate here means it cannot re-enter gluconeogenesis directly, making PDC regulation critical.

Explanation
PDC is a massive complex made of three enzyme components (E1: pyruvate decarboxylase, E2: dihydrolipoyl transacetylase, E3: dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase) working in an ordered sequence. Understanding the cofactor roles is high-yield: TPP (derived from thiamine, Vitamin B₁) decarboxylates pyruvate; lipoic acid swings between the enzyme active sites carrying the intermediate and gets oxidised in the process; CoA accepts the acetyl group to form Acetyl-CoA; FAD reoxidises lipoic acid; NAD⁺ accepts electrons from FADH₂ to regenerate FAD and produce NADH.

PDC regulation is by covalent modification: PDC Kinase phosphorylates and inactivates the complex; PDC Phosphatase dephosphorylates and activates it. This is why Ca²⁺ activates PDC — Ca²⁺ activates PDC Phosphatase, shifting the complex to its active dephosphorylated form. This is particularly relevant in muscle during exercise, where Ca²⁺ release during contraction simultaneously stimulates muscle work and activates PDC to provide the fuel.

Inter-pathway link: GluconeogenesisPDC's irreversibility is why even-chain fatty acids cannot be gluconeogenic — Acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to pyruvate, so the 2 carbons entering via Acetyl-CoA cannot contribute net carbon to glucose. Fatty Acid SynthesisAcetyl-CoA produced by PDC can be used for fatty acid synthesis (in the cytosol, after export as citrate). High carbohydrate intake drives PDC activity and therefore lipogenesis.

Inhibitors
⊖ NADH — signals ETC saturation; no need for more substrate ⊖ Succinyl-CoA — downstream product feedback ⊖ ATP — high energy state ⊖ Citrate (product inhibition)
Explanation
This is the committed entry step — the point where Acetyl-CoA's two carbons are incorporated into the cycle. Citrate Synthase condenses Acetyl-CoA with OAA in a reaction that is highly exergonic and therefore essentially irreversible under cellular conditions.

The logic behind its inhibitors is unified: NADH, succinyl-CoA, ATP, and citrate all signal that the cycle's products are already abundant — the cell is energy-replete and does not need more flux through the pathway. When these signals are all low (active muscle, fasted liver), Citrate Synthase runs freely.

Note on OAA availability: The cycle's speed is also limited by how much OAA is present. OAA levels fall when gluconeogenesis is active (OAA is diverted to PEPCK) and rise during the fed state. This is one reason why fat oxidation in the complete absence of carbohydrates is inefficient — insufficient OAA to condense with all the Acetyl-CoA generated, leading to ketone body formation instead.

Inter-pathway link: Fatty Acid SynthesisCitrate produced here can be exported from the mitochondria to the cytosol, where it is cleaved by ATP-Citrate Lyase back into Acetyl-CoA and OAA. The cytosolic Acetyl-CoA feeds fatty acid synthesis. GluconeogenesisCitrate in the cytosol is an inhibitor of PFK-1, slowing glycolysis when the TCA cycle is running well-fed — a cross-pathway signal preventing futile cycling.

Explanation
Aconitase isomerises citrate to isocitrate by removing and re-adding water — first dehydrating citrate to the intermediate cis-Aconitate, then rehydrating to produce isocitrate. This repositions the hydroxyl group to make isocitrate a suitable substrate for oxidative decarboxylation in step 3.

Iron-sulfur cluster: Aconitase contains a [4Fe-4S] cluster that is essential for its catalytic activity. This makes Aconitase sensitive to oxidative stress — reactive oxygen species can damage the iron-sulfur cluster, inactivating the enzyme and slowing the TCA cycle.

Fluoroacetate poisoning: Fluoroacetate (used in rodenticide) is converted in the cell to fluorocitrate, which is a potent inhibitor of Aconitase. The TCA cycle blocks at step 2, citrate accumulates massively, and energy production collapses — lethal in sufficient doses. This is a high-yield clinical correlation.

Explanation
Isocitrate Dehydrogenase catalyses an oxidative decarboxylation — isocitrate is simultaneously oxidised (losing electrons to NAD⁺) and decarboxylated (losing CO₂), producing α-Ketoglutarate. This is a large energy-releasing step with a strongly negative ΔG, making it irreversible.

NADH as the universal brake: When NADH rises (either from active TCA cycling or from fatty acid oxidation feeding NADH into the ETC), it inhibits Isocitrate Dehydrogenase, slowing the cycle. This prevents NADH from accumulating to levels that would overwhelm the ETC and generate excessive reactive oxygen species.

Ca²⁺ activation is physiologically elegant: during muscle contraction, Ca²⁺ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum simultaneously triggers muscle work (via troponin) and activates Isocitrate Dehydrogenase, increasing ATP production to match the increased demand.

Inter-pathway link: Amino Acid Metabolismα-Ketoglutarate is the direct precursor of glutamate (via transamination or glutamate dehydrogenase). Glutamate is the major nitrogen carrier in amino acid metabolism and feeds into many amino acid biosynthetic pathways.

Explanation
α-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase Complex operates by exactly the same mechanism as PDC — oxidative decarboxylation using the same five cofactors in the same sequence. This is not a coincidence: these reactions share the same chemical challenge (removing CO₂ from an α-keto acid and linking the resulting group to CoA), and evolution solved it the same way twice.

The arsenic connection is clinically important: arsenic and arsenite inhibit this complex (and PDC) by binding to the sulfhydryl groups of lipoic acid, preventing it from cycling between its oxidised and reduced forms. This effectively blocks both oxidative decarboxylation steps, collapsing TCA cycle flux and causing severe cellular energy failure — the basis of arsenic toxicity.

Inter-pathway link: Haem SynthesisSuccinyl-CoA produced here condenses with glycine (catalysed by ALA Synthase, the rate-limiting step of haem synthesis) to form δ-Aminolevulinate (ALA), the first committed step of porphyrin and haem synthesis. Explanation
The only step in the TCA cycle that directly generates a high-energy nucleotide — GTP, not ATP. GTP is readily interconvertible with ATP via Nucleoside Diphosphate Kinase (GTP + ADP ⇌ GDP + ATP), so this effectively represents 1 ATP per turn in energetic terms.

The energy comes from breaking the high-energy thioester bond of Succinyl-CoA — the same type of bond used at step 7 of glycolysis (1,3-BPG → 3-PG) — and coupling the released energy to GTP synthesis. This is substrate-level phosphorylation, producing energy directly without the electron transport chain.

CoA is released here and is free to participate in other reactions — it returns to step 4 to accept the next acyl group.

Explanation
Succinate Dehydrogenase is unique among TCA cycle enzymes: it is embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane as Complex II of the electron transport chain, rather than dissolved in the matrix. This means FADH₂ does not need to be transported — it immediately passes its electrons to ubiquinone (CoQ) within the membrane, directly feeding the ETC.

FAD is used instead of NAD⁺ here because the oxidation of succinate to fumarate is not energetically sufficient to reduce NAD⁺ (which requires more energy). FAD, with its lower reduction potential, can accept electrons from this reaction where NAD⁺ cannot — a good example of how coenzyme choice is dictated by thermodynamics, not convenience.

Malonate inhibition is the classic competitive inhibition example in biochemistry — malonate is structurally similar to succinate (both are dicarboxylates) and competes for the active site without being oxidised.

Explanation
A simple hydration reaction — water is added across the double bond of fumarate to produce L-Malate. Fumarase is stereospecific: it produces only the L-form of malate, not the D-form. No energy is generated or consumed. This step primarily exists to set up the final oxidation in step 8.

Worth knowing: fumarate is also produced in the urea cycle (from argininosuccinate → arginine + fumarate) and in purine synthesis. The fumarate generated in these pathways enters the TCA cycle at this step, connecting nitrogen metabolism to energy metabolism — a cross-pathway link examiners like to test.

Inter-pathway link: Urea CycleFumarate is released in the urea cycle when argininosuccinate is cleaved to arginine + fumarate. This fumarate enters the TCA cycle at step 7, linking the urea cycle and TCA cycle — sometimes called the 'Krebs bicycle'.

Reaction Type

Explanation
The final step of the cycle — malate is oxidised to regenerate oxaloacetate, producing the third NADH of the turn. The regenerated OAA immediately re-enters step 1 to condense with the next Acetyl-CoA, completing the cycle.

This reaction is thermodynamically unfavourable under standard conditions (ΔG°' is positive) — OAA is a high-energy compound that doesn't form easily. In the cell, it proceeds because OAA is continuously removed by Citrate Synthase (step 1), driving the reaction forward by Le Chatelier's principle. This is a perfect example of how metabolic pathways are pulled forward by coupling to subsequent favourable reactions.

Malate-Aspartate Shuttle: Malate Dehydrogenase exists in both the mitochondrial matrix and the cytosol. The cytosolic enzyme plays a key role in the malate-aspartate shuttle — the primary mechanism by which cytosolic NADH (from glycolysis step 6) transfers its electrons into the mitochondria for ETC processing.

Inter-pathway link: GluconeogenesisOAA regenerated here can be diverted to gluconeogenesis: exported as malate (or aspartate) from the mitochondria, reoxidised to OAA in the cytosol, then converted to PEP by PEPCK. This is why the TCA cycle feeds gluconeogenesis during fasting. Malate-Aspartate ShuttleCytosolic NADH is converted to malate by cytosolic Malate Dehydrogenase running in reverse. Malate crosses the inner mitochondrial membrane and is reoxidised to OAA by mitochondrial Malate Dehydrogenase, regenerating mitochondrial NADH for the ETC.
Regulatory Overview
The Krebs cycle is regulated at three irreversible steps — Citrate Synthase (step 1), Isocitrate Dehydrogenase (step 3), and α-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase Complex (step 4). All three respond to the same fundamental signals: the cell's energy state (NADH/NAD⁺ ratio and ATP/ADP ratio) and Ca²⁺ in excitable tissues. The dominant theme is product inhibition by NADH — when NADH accumulates because the ETC cannot keep up, all three regulatory enzymes slow down, pulling the cycle back to match the pace the ETC can sustain. Flooding the ETC with more electrons than it can process would generate reactive oxygen species and damage the cell.

Anaplerosis — Replenishing Cycle Intermediates
Because TCA cycle intermediates are constantly being drawn off for biosynthesis (α-ketoglutarate for amino acids, succinyl-CoA for haem, OAA for gluconeogenesis, citrate for fatty acid synthesis), the cycle would gradually deplete its own intermediates and grind to a halt without replenishment. Anaplerotic reactions top up the pool of cycle intermediates, keeping the cycle running even when substrates are being diverted. The most important entry points:
→ OAA
Pyruvate Carboxylase — the most important anaplerotic reaction. Pyruvate + CO₂ + ATP → OAA. Active during gluconeogenesis and when OAA falls. Requires biotin and Acetyl-CoA as activator.
→ α-KG
Glutamate Dehydrogenase / Transaminases — amino acids (especially glutamate) are transaminated or deaminated to produce α-ketoglutarate, replenishing the cycle at step 3's product. Links amino acid catabolism to energy production.
→ Succinyl-CoA
Propionyl-CoA Carboxylase — odd-chain fatty acid β-oxidation produces propionyl-CoA, converted via methylmalonyl-CoA (requiring Vitamin B₁₂) to succinyl-CoA. This is why odd-chain fatty acids — unlike even-chain — can contribute to gluconeogenesis.
→ Fumarate
Urea Cycle / Purine Synthesis — fumarate released in the urea cycle (from argininosuccinate cleavage) and in purine nucleotide synthesis enters the TCA cycle at step 7, linking nitrogen disposal to energy metabolism.

Thiamine (Vitamin B₁) Deficiency — Wernicke's Encephalopathy & Beriberi
Affected enzymes: PDC + α-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase Complex (both require TPP)
Nutritional DeficiencyHigh-Yield

Thiamine (Vitamin B₁) is the precursor of Thiamine Pyrophosphate (TPP), an essential cofactor for both PDC and α-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase Complex. Without TPP, both decarboxylation steps fail — pyruvate cannot enter the TCA cycle as Acetyl-CoA, and α-ketoglutarate cannot proceed to succinyl-CoA. The entire TCA cycle slows dramatically.

Pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate accumulate. Backed-up pyruvate is reduced to lactate (lactic acidosis) or transaminated to alanine. ATP production collapses because the primary fuel source for the ETC is gone. The brain is the first to suffer — it is almost entirely dependent on glucose oxidation via PDC and the TCA cycle, with minimal capacity for alternative fuels.

Wernicke's encephalopathy: the classic triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia (eye movement abnormalities), and ataxia (gait disturbance) results from selective damage to thiamine-dependent brain regions (mamillary bodies, thalamus, brainstem). If untreated, it progresses to Korsakoff syndrome with irreversible memory loss.

Beriberi: affects peripheral nerves (dry beriberi: neuropathy) and the cardiovascular system (wet beriberi: high-output heart failure from vasodilation and fluid retention). Cause: polished rice diet (thiamine in the husk, removed by polishing), chronic alcohol abuse (poor absorption + poor diet), prolonged parenteral nutrition without supplementation.
Arsenic Poisoning
Mechanism: Arsenic binds lipoic acid sulfhydryl groups → inhibits PDC + α-KG Dehydrogenase Complex
ToxicMulti-System

Arsenite (As³⁺) has a high affinity for sulfhydryl (−SH) groups. Lipoic acid — the swinging arm cofactor that carries the acyl intermediate between active sites in both PDC and α-KG Dehydrogenase Complex — contains two sulfhydryl groups in its reduced (active) form. Arsenite binds these sulfhydryls and locks lipoic acid in an inactive configuration, preventing it from cycling between its oxidised and reduced states.

The result is identical to the effect of thiamine deficiency at the metabolic level — both PDC and α-KG Dehydrogenase Complex are blocked, TCA cycle flux collapses, pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate accumulate, and cellular energy production fails. The difference is mechanism: thiamine deficiency removes a cofactor; arsenite inactivates an existing one.

Clinically: multi-system toxicity affecting skin (Mees' lines on nails, hyperpigmentation), peripheral nerves (neuropathy), GI tract (vomiting, diarrhoea), and cardiovascular system. Chronic low-dose exposure causes cancer. Dimercaprol (BAL) and DMSA are chelating agents used in treatment — they compete for arsenic binding, displacing it from lipoic acid's sulfhydryls.
Fluoroacetate Poisoning
Mechanism: Fluorocitrate inhibits Aconitase (Step 2) → citrate accumulates
Toxic (Rodenticide)Suicide Substrate

Fluoroacetate is a naturally occurring plant toxin and a used rodenticide. Once inside the cell, it undergoes a lethal synthesis: it condenses with oxaloacetate via Citrate Synthase (step 1) to form fluorocitrate — a molecule that looks like citrate but cannot be processed by Aconitase. Fluorocitrate binds tightly to Aconitase's active site and inhibits it competitively and irreversibly.

The TCA cycle blocks completely at step 2. Citrate accumulates massively in the mitochondria and overflows into the blood and urine — a hallmark laboratory finding. All downstream steps stop, no NADH or FADH₂ is generated, the ETC starves of electrons, and ATP synthesis collapses. The brain and heart are most affected because of their high energy demands.

This is sometimes called a "lethal synthesis" — the body's own enzymes (Citrate Synthase) convert the compound into a toxic metabolite, making it paradoxically more dangerous than if it were simply inert.
Succinyl-CoA & Haem Synthesis — Porphyrias
Link: Succinyl-CoA (Step 4 product) + Glycine → ALA → Haem
Metabolic LinkClinically Important

Succinyl-CoA is not just a TCA cycle intermediate — it is the committed entry point for haem biosynthesis. ALA Synthase (the rate-limiting enzyme of haem synthesis) condenses succinyl-CoA with glycine to form δ-Aminolevulinate (ALA), the first committed porphyrin precursor.

Porphyrias are a group of disorders caused by enzyme defects along the haem synthesis pathway downstream of ALA. When these enzymes are deficient, ALA and other porphyrin precursors accumulate. Acute intermittent porphyria (AIP) — deficiency of Porphobilinogen Deaminase — causes episodic abdominal pain, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and dark urine triggered by certain drugs (barbiturates, alcohol), fasting, or hormonal changes. Understanding that succinyl-CoA is the TCA cycle's contribution to this pathway explains why states of high TCA cycle activity (fed state, high protein intake) can paradoxically worsen porphyrias by generating more ALA.
