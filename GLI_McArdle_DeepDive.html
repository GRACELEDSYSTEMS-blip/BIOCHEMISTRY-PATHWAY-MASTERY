What Is This Disease?
A Muscle That Cannot Open Its Own Fuel Tank

Every muscle cell stores a small reserve of glycogen — a compact form of glucose that can be rapidly broken down when the muscle needs to work hard. When you sprint up stairs, lift a heavy object, or sustain any intense exercise, your muscle cells need glucose immediately. The fastest source is their own glycogen. The enzyme that breaks this glycogen down is called Muscle Glycogen Phosphorylase — also known as myophosphorylase.

In McArdle disease, myophosphorylase is absent. The glycogen is there — the muscle cells are packed with it, actually more than normal because it can never be used. But the enzyme that unlocks it is missing. The muscle cell is sitting on a full tank of fuel with no way to open the tap.

The moment exercise begins and ATP demand surges, the muscle tries to break down its glycogen — and fails. The cell is forced to rely entirely on other fuel sources: circulating blood glucose (which arrives slowly from the bloodstream), fatty acids (which are too slow for high-intensity work), and the small amount of ATP and phosphocreatine already in the cell. These alternative sources cannot keep up with intense demand. The muscle runs out of energy rapidly, producing the characteristic cramps and fatigue of McArdle disease.

⛽ The Fuel Tank Analogy
Imagine a racing car with a full petrol tank but a broken fuel pump. The engine starts, burns through what's in the carburettor, and then sputters to a halt — not because there's no fuel, but because it can't be delivered. If the driver slows down and waits, fuel from a slower backup system (an auxiliary tank, like circulating fatty acids and blood glucose) trickles in and the car can run again at reduced speed. That's McArdle disease during exercise — the main fuel source is there but inaccessible, and a slower backup keeps things going at low intensity.

What makes McArdle disease conceptually elegant — and important for exams — is what it does NOT affect. Liver phosphorylase is a completely different enzyme encoded by a different gene (PYGL, not PYGM). The liver breaks down glycogen normally, releases glucose normally, and maintains blood glucose perfectly between meals. This is why McArdle patients have completely normal fasting blood glucose — one of the most important distinguishing features of this disease.

The Core Concept
Why Only Muscle? The Isoenzyme Story

Glycogen Phosphorylase is not a single enzyme — it exists as three distinct isoforms encoded by three different genes, each expressed in different tissues:

Muscle isoform (myophosphorylase) — PYGM gene, chromosome 11q13: expressed in skeletal muscle. This is the one missing in McArdle disease.

Liver isoform (liver phosphorylase) — PYGL gene: expressed in the liver. This breaks down liver glycogen to maintain blood glucose. Completely normal in McArdle disease. Deficient in Hers disease (GSD Type VI).

Brain isoform — PYGB gene: expressed in brain and heart. Also normal in McArdle disease.

This isoform specificity is why McArdle disease is so clean in its presentation — only skeletal muscle is affected, and only during exercise. The liver is completely normal, the heart is unaffected, and brain metabolism is fine. The disease is entirely confined to the one tissue that expresses only myophosphorylase.

🔑
The single most important distinguishing fact about McArdle disease: fasting blood glucose is completely normal because liver phosphorylase (a different gene, a different enzyme) is unaffected. This immediately separates McArdle from all hepatic GSDs (Von Gierke, Cori, Hers) where hypoglycaemia is a central feature.
Where It Fits in the Pathway
The First Step of Muscle Glycogenolysis — Blocked

In normal muscle glycogenolysis, the sequence is: Glycogen + Pᵢ → Glucose-1-phosphate (via Glycogen Phosphorylase) → Glucose-6-phosphate (via Phosphoglucomutase) → enters glycolysis → ATP. This pathway is what gives muscles the burst capacity for intense exercise.

In McArdle disease, the very first step is blocked. Glycogen Phosphorylase cannot cleave glucose units from glycogen. Everything downstream from that step — Phosphoglucomutase, glycolysis, ATP production from glycogen — is intact and normal. The block is at the entry point. Glycogen accumulates because it is continuously synthesised (Glycogen Synthase is normal) but can never be broken down.

The muscle therefore has an unusual metabolic profile during exercise: glycogen builds up progressively (because synthesis continues but breakdown is zero), while blood glucose, fatty acid oxidation, and the creatine phosphate system carry all the burden. At low exercise intensities these can compensate. At high intensities, they cannot, and the muscle cramps.

Plain English Summary
At rest, everything is normal — the muscle doesn't need its glycogen, blood glucose keeps things ticking over, and there are no symptoms. The problem only appears when exercise demands more energy than the blood can supply fast enough. Then the muscle tries to break down its glycogen, finds it can't, and starts to fail. The faster and harder the exercise, the sooner it fails.
Step-by-Step Mechanism
What Happens in the Muscle During Exercise

To understand McArdle disease mechanistically, you need to understand what normally drives muscle glycogenolysis during exercise, and what happens when that first step is blocked. The story unfolds in three phases: the normal energy sequence, what goes wrong in McArdle, and why the "second wind" occurs.

Phase 1 — What Normally Happens at the Start of Exercise
1
Normal First Response
ATP and Phosphocreatine — the immediate buffer (0–10 seconds)
At the very start of exercise, ATP is consumed by myosin ATPase for muscle contraction. The immediate buffer is phosphocreatine (PCr), which donates its phosphate group to ADP to regenerate ATP via Creatine Kinase (CK). This works for about 5–10 seconds of maximum intensity exercise. In McArdle disease, this step is completely normal — both the PCr store and creatine kinase are intact.
PCr + ADP → Creatine + ATP (via Creatine Kinase) — NORMAL in McArdle
2
Normal Second Response
Glycogenolysis — the rapid glycogen fuel (10 seconds to several minutes)
As PCr is depleted, normally the muscle rapidly activates myophosphorylase. Calcium ions released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum during contraction activate Phosphorylase Kinase (via its calmodulin subunit), which converts Phosphorylase b → active Phosphorylase a. Additionally, AMP (rising as ATP is consumed) directly allosterically activates Phosphorylase b. Both signals ensure glycogenolysis accelerates instantly with exercise. In McArdle disease, myophosphorylase is absent — this entire step is missing. After PCr is used up, the muscle has no fast glycogen fuel source.
Ca²⁺ → Phosphorylase Kinase → Phosphorylase b → a; AMP directly activates Phosphorylase b — BLOCKED in McArdle
3
Normal Third Response
Oxidative phosphorylation — sustained aerobic fuel (minutes to hours)
For sustained moderate exercise, the muscle shifts to oxidative phosphorylation — fatty acid oxidation and the TCA cycle provide NADH and FADH₂ for the ETC to make ATP. This is slower but sustainable. Circulating blood glucose also contributes via glycolysis. In McArdle disease, oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation are completely intact. This is why McArdle patients can perform sustained low-intensity exercise (e.g. walking) reasonably well — their aerobic capacity is functional.
Phase 2 — What Goes Wrong in McArdle Disease
A
Primary Block
Myophosphorylase absent → glycogen cannot be broken down
As soon as the PCr buffer runs out (within the first 10–30 seconds of intense exercise), the muscle desperately needs glycogen-derived glucose. Myophosphorylase is activated normally by Ca²⁺ and AMP — but since the enzyme protein itself is absent, no Glucose-1-phosphate is produced. Glycogen accumulates progressively inside the muscle cells. The glycogen is visible on muscle biopsy as abnormal subsarcolemmal (just under the cell membrane) glycogen deposits — a characteristic finding on electron microscopy.
PYGM gene mutation → no myophosphorylase protein → glycogen cannot be phosphorolysed → G1P = 0
B
Energy Crisis
ATP falls → ADP and AMP accumulate → muscle cannot contract
With glycogenolysis blocked, ATP production from glycolysis (which depends on the G6P from glycogen breakdown) is severely limited during high-intensity exercise. ATP falls, ADP rises, and through the Adenylate Kinase reaction (2 ADP → ATP + AMP), AMP accumulates. The muscle cell cannot generate enough ATP to sustain contraction. Sarcomeres seize up in a contracted state — the muscle stiffens and cramps. This is not a normal cramp from electrolyte imbalance; it is a contracture — the muscle literally locks up because it cannot generate enough ATP to release the actin-myosin cross-bridges.
No G1P → no G6P → no glycolytic ATP → 2ADP → ATP + AMP (adenylate kinase) → AMP accumulates
C
Diagnostic Consequence
No lactate produced during ischaemic exercise → diagnostic marker
In a normal person exercising under ischaemia (blocked blood flow), glycogen → G6P → glycolysis → pyruvate → lactate. Venous lactate rises sharply. In McArdle disease, no glycogen is broken down → no G6P → no glycolytic substrate from glycogen → no pyruvate → no lactate rise. Venous lactate stays flat. This is the basis of the ischaemic forearm exercise test (or the non-ischaemic version) — the most important diagnostic test for McArdle disease. Ammonia still rises normally (from the purine nucleotide cycle — AMP → IMP + NH₃, which doesn't require glycolysis), confirming the patient actually exercised. Ammonia up, lactate flat = McArdle pattern.
No G6P from glycogen → no glycolytic flux → pyruvate = 0 → lactate = 0 during forearm test; AMP → IMP + NH₃ → ammonia rises normally
D
Muscle Damage
Severe exercise → sustained contracture → muscle fibre necrosis → myoglobinuria
If the patient pushes through the cramps and continues intense exercise, the sustained contracture and ATP depletion damage muscle fibres. The muscle cell membrane becomes leaky — intracellular proteins spill into the blood. Creatine Kinase (CK) rises dramatically in the blood (sometimes >10,000 U/L; normal <200). Myoglobin — the oxygen-carrying protein of muscle — is released from damaged fibres. It is filtered by the kidneys, turning the urine dark red-brown (resembling cola or dark tea). This is myoglobinuria. If enough myoglobin is released, it precipitates in the renal tubules (especially in acidic, concentrated urine) and causes acute kidney injury (AKI) — the most dangerous complication of McArdle disease.
Contracture → sustained ATP depletion → sarcolemmal damage → CK release → myoglobin release → myoglobinuria → AKI risk
The Pathognomonic Phenomenon
The "Second Wind" — What It Is and Why It Happens

The "second wind" is one of the most clinically important and conceptually beautiful features of McArdle disease — and it is pathognomonic. No other disease produces it in quite the same way.

Here is what happens: A McArdle patient begins exercise. Within the first 5–10 minutes, they develop severe muscle pain, weakness, and cramps — the classic early symptoms. If they slow down or briefly rest at this point (without stopping completely), something remarkable happens: after 5–10 minutes, the symptoms improve significantly and they can continue exercising at a moderate level with much less discomfort. This is the second wind.

🌊 Why the Second Wind Occurs
The first phase (0–10 min): The muscle is working hard, PCr is depleted, and it desperately needs glycogen — which it cannot access. The only other fuel is blood glucose and fatty acids, which arrive slowly. There is a mismatch between demand (high) and supply (low). The muscle cramps.

The transition: When the patient slows down, muscle workload drops. The cardiovascular system has been ramping up throughout — heart rate and cardiac output have increased, skeletal muscle blood flow has risen, and the redistribution of blood to working muscles has been optimising. Now, with lower demand, circulating blood glucose and fatty acids — arriving at the muscle in larger quantities because of the increased blood flow — can finally match the lower demand.

The second wind (after 10 min): The muscle has shifted its fuel dependence entirely to oxidative metabolism — fatty acids and blood glucose. These sources, while slower than glycogen, are sufficient for low-to-moderate intensity exercise. The patient feels they can "go again." Importantly, this fuel shift takes time — hence the characteristic 10-minute window of early symptoms.

The second wind is clinically important because it confirms the diagnosis — no other muscle disease has this exact pattern. It also has practical management implications: teaching McArdle patients to warm up slowly, respect the early symptom window, and not push through the first-phase cramps dramatically reduces their risk of myoglobinuria and AKI.

Technical Basis of the Second Wind
Evidence from metabolic studies during the second wind shows:
— Blood glucose uptake by working muscle increases significantly
— Free fatty acid oxidation increases
— Heart rate actually DECREASES at the same workload during the second wind (less sympathetic activation needed because the metabolic crisis is resolving)
— This heart rate drop during the second wind is measurable and has been proposed as an objective marker of the phenomenon
— Glycogen in the muscle does NOT decrease (confirming no glycogenolysis is occurring even during the second wind)
What Happens During Exercise in McArdle Disease — Timeline
0 – 30 sec PCr buffer + ATP stores — brief tolerance
Phosphocreatine donates phosphate to regenerate ATP. The patient feels normal. This phase is completely normal in McArdle disease — it doesn't require glycogen. Duration depends on exercise intensity: maximal sprint exhausts PCr in seconds; moderate exercise may sustain this phase longer.
30 sec – 10 min Crisis phase — cramps, pain, fatigue
PCr is exhausted. Normally, glycogenolysis would kick in here — but myophosphorylase is absent. ATP falls. AMP accumulates. Muscle fibres are in energy crisis. Symptoms: severe muscle pain, stiffness, weakness. The muscle may lock in contracture. If the patient pushes through, myoglobinuria risk is high. This is the most dangerous phase. The correct response is to slow down, not to push harder.
10 – 20 min Second wind — symptoms improve
If the patient slows down during the crisis phase, the cardiovascular adaptation (increased blood flow to muscle) combined with lowered work demand allows fatty acid oxidation and blood glucose to meet demand. Symptoms improve markedly. The patient can resume moderate exercise with much greater comfort. Heart rate paradoxically decreases at the same workload.
If pushed hard again Myoglobinuria risk — dark urine, AKI
If the patient ignores cramps and pushes through into sustained intense exercise, muscle fibre necrosis occurs. CK releases into blood. Myoglobin turns urine dark red-brown. AKI from myoglobin precipitation in renal tubules. Requires emergency management: IV fluids (very high volumes), urine alkalinisation to prevent myoglobin precipitation, and renal monitoring.
Clinical Presentation
How McArdle Disease Presents

McArdle disease typically presents in childhood or early adulthood, though it is often not diagnosed until later — many patients spend years being told they are unfit or anxious about exercise. The key to diagnosis is the very specific pattern: symptoms appear only during exercise, resolve with rest, and are absent at rest and during fasting.

The classic patient is a young person who has always been intolerant of strenuous exercise, particularly sudden intense bursts (sprinting, heavy lifting, stair climbing), and who notices they can do better if they start slowly.

💪
Exercise Intolerance & Early Fatigue
Cardinal symptom · Present in all patients
The defining feature. Patients fatigue abnormally quickly during any exercise that requires significant sustained effort — running, cycling, swimming, carrying heavy loads, or even climbing stairs. The fatigue is out of proportion to the effort and is often described as an overwhelming muscle weakness rather than the general tiredness healthy people feel. It is specifically worse in the first 10 minutes of exercise before the second wind, and worse for isometric (pushing against a fixed object) than isotonic exercise. Rest reliably relieves it.
No G1P from glycogen → no glycolytic ATP for burst → muscles fatigue before cardiovascular system catches up
😣
Exercise-Induced Muscle Pain & Cramps
Painful contractures · Not a normal cramp
During the crisis phase (typically 2–10 minutes into intense exercise), patients develop severe muscle pain and stiffness. Unlike the dull fatigue of deconditioning, this is an acute, intense, and localised pain in the working muscles. The muscle may become palpably hard — a contracture (the muscle locks in contraction because there is insufficient ATP to release the actin-myosin cross-bridges). This is genuinely painful and forces the patient to stop. It should never be pushed through — doing so risks myoglobinuria.
ATP depletion → actin-myosin cross-bridges cannot release → contracture → severe pain → myoglobinuria if ignored
🌬️
The "Second Wind" Phenomenon
Pathognomonic · Diagnostic clue
After slowing during the crisis phase, patients experience a striking improvement in symptoms after 5–10 minutes — the "second wind." They can then continue exercising at a moderate level with significantly less pain and fatigue. This is pathognomonic — no other muscle disease reliably produces this pattern. It reflects the transition from (failed) glycogen-based fuel to successful oxidative metabolism (fatty acids + blood glucose). Patients often learn this pattern intuitively and describe "warming up carefully" before they can exercise.
Slow down → cardiovascular adaptation increases blood flow → fatty acid oxidation + blood glucose meets reduced demand → symptoms improve
🔴
Myoglobinuria — Dark Urine
50–75% of patients · AKI risk
After episodes of severe exercise or sustained contracture, myoglobin released from damaged muscle fibres is filtered into urine, turning it dark red, brown, or cola-coloured. This is a medical emergency — myoglobin precipitates in renal tubules, especially in concentrated or acidic urine, causing tubular obstruction and acute kidney injury. Not every episode of myoglobinuria causes AKI, but severe or repeated episodes can lead to significant renal damage. Any episode of dark urine after exercise requires immediate medical attention and aggressive IV fluid resuscitation.
Sustained contracture → fibre necrosis → myoglobin → urine → dark urine; renal tubule precipitation → AKI risk
📈
Elevated Resting CK
Between episodes · Baseline elevation
Even between exercise episodes, when the patient is asymptomatic, serum creatine kinase (CK) is elevated — often 3–10× normal (normal: <200 U/L; McArdle baseline: typically 500–3000 U/L). This reflects low-level ongoing muscle fibre damage from the cumulative stress of using muscles without adequate glycogen fuel. During and after exercise episodes, CK can rise to 10,000–100,000 U/L or higher. The persistent baseline elevation is a useful screening clue in a patient with exercise intolerance — an unexplained elevated CK in a young person always warrants investigation.
Chronic inadequate ATP supply → low-level sarcolemmal stress → CK leaks into blood even at rest
✅
Normal Fasting Blood Glucose
Critical distinguishing feature
Fasting blood glucose is completely normal in McArdle disease. This is the single most important feature that distinguishes it from hepatic GSDs. The liver breaks down its glycogen normally via liver phosphorylase (a different enzyme), maintains gluconeogenesis normally, and exports free glucose normally via Glucose-6-phosphatase. Patients can fast for a full day without any metabolic abnormality. There is no lactic acidosis at rest, no hypertriglyceridaemia from metabolic overflow, no hyperuricaemia. The metabolic profile at rest is essentially normal.
Liver phosphorylase (PYGL) intact → normal glycogenolysis → G6P → G6Pase → free glucose → normal fasting glucose
💉
No Venous Lactate Rise on Exercise Testing
Diagnostic hallmark · Forearm test
During the ischaemic forearm exercise test (or non-ischaemic version), venous lactate fails to rise while venous ammonia rises normally. This pattern is diagnostic. Normal subjects generate lactate from muscle glycolysis during exercise (glycogen → G6P → glycolysis → lactate). In McArdle, no glycogen is broken down → no glycolytic substrate → no lactate. Ammonia rises (from AMP degradation via the purine nucleotide cycle — an AMP deaminase reaction that does not need glycolysis), confirming the patient genuinely exercised. Lactate flat + ammonia up = glycogen breakdown defect at or before glycolysis.
No G1P → no G6P → no glycolysis → no lactate; AMP → IMP + NH₃ (AMP deaminase) → ammonia rises
🔬
Fixed Proximal Weakness (In Some Patients)
Late-onset · Not universal
In a minority of patients — usually those diagnosed late or with longstanding poorly managed disease — fixed proximal muscle weakness develops with age, independent of exercise. This is from chronic cumulative muscle damage. The weakness is similar to a limb-girdle muscular dystrophy pattern — affecting shoulder and hip girdle muscles. It does not respond to rest (unlike exercise-induced weakness). This late-onset fixed weakness can complicate diagnosis in older patients, as it resembles other myopathies. Muscle biopsy in these patients may show not only glycogen accumulation but also fibre necrosis and regeneration.
Cumulative exercise-induced damage → fibre necrosis → incomplete regeneration → fixed weakness

🧪 The Forearm Exercise Test — Understanding What It Measures

The test: The patient squeezes a hand grip repeatedly for 1–2 minutes while a blood pressure cuff occludes venous drainage (ischaemic version) or without occlusion (non-ischaemic — safer and equally informative). Venous blood is sampled from the antecubital vein at baseline and at 1, 3, 6, and 10 minutes after exercise. Both lactate and ammonia are measured.


Why ischaemia? Blocking venous drainage forces the muscle to rely entirely on anaerobic glycolysis for ATP — it cannot use oxidative phosphorylation because that would exhaust oxygen without replenishment. This makes the test specifically sensitive to glycolytic defects. However, the non-ischaemic version is now preferred because ischaemia can risk severe contracture and rhabdomyolysis in McArdle patients.


What you're measuring: Lactate tells you whether glycolysis from glycogen is working. Ammonia tells you whether the patient genuinely exercised (AMP deaminase is activated by exercise regardless of glycogen availability). If the patient fails to exercise adequately, both lactate AND ammonia stay flat — a falsely negative test that must be interpreted carefully.

Normal Subject
Lactate: rises 3–5× above baseline
Ammonia: rises 3–5× above baseline
Interpretation: Glycolysis working normally; patient exercised
McArdle Disease
Lactate: flat — minimal or no rise
Ammonia: rises normally
Interpretation: Glycolysis blocked despite genuine exercise; glycogen breakdown defect confirmed
High-Yield Exam Intelligence
The Traps That Catch Students in McArdle Exam Questions

McArdle disease appears in exams primarily as clinical vignettes (young person with exercise intolerance and dark urine) and as R&A questions testing the mechanism of the second wind, the forearm test, and comparisons with other diseases. These are the specific traps to watch for.

The Most Important Exam Traps
⚠️ Classic Trap 1
"McArdle disease causes hypoglycaemia during fasting"
This is completely wrong — and it is the most commonly confused point. McArdle disease has normal fasting blood glucose. The disease affects only muscle phosphorylase — liver phosphorylase is a completely different enzyme (PYGL gene) and is fully intact. The liver breaks down glycogen, maintains gluconeogenesis, and exports free glucose exactly as in a healthy person. Students confuse this because other GSDs (Von Gierke, Cori, Hers) do cause hypoglycaemia. McArdle is the exception — it is a pure muscle disease with zero hepatic involvement.
⚠️ Classic Trap 2
"The forearm test shows flat lactate AND flat ammonia"
Wrong — ammonia rises normally in McArdle disease. Ammonia is produced by the purine nucleotide cycle — AMP deaminase converts AMP → IMP + NH₃. This reaction is activated by exercise (AMP accumulation) and does not require glycolysis or glycogen breakdown. It is completely intact in McArdle disease. Flat lactate + flat ammonia together suggests either: (a) the patient did not actually exercise adequately (falsely negative test), or (b) a defect in the purine nucleotide cycle (AMP deaminase deficiency — a different, much milder condition). In McArdle: lactate flat, ammonia rises. That's the diagnostic pattern.
⚠️ Classic Trap 3
"McArdle disease causes rhabdomyolysis at rest"
Wrong — rhabdomyolysis in McArdle is exercise-induced, not spontaneous. At rest, the muscle does not need glycogen — basal ATP demand is met by oxidative phosphorylation (fatty acids, blood glucose), which is fully intact. Muscle damage only occurs when the patient exercises beyond the capacity of the alternative fuel sources — i.e. during intense or sustained exercise. Some other myopathies (inflammatory myopathies, metabolic myopathies, statin-induced rhabdomyolysis) can cause resting or spontaneous muscle breakdown. McArdle is not one of them. The elevated resting CK reflects background low-level stress, not active rhabdomyolysis.
💡 Key Insight 1
Why the muscle cannot use its glycogen even at low exercise intensity
Students sometimes assume McArdle patients are completely unable to exercise — this is wrong. At low intensities, oxidative phosphorylation from fatty acids and blood glucose is sufficient, and exercise is tolerable. The problem is specifically with the transition from rest to exercise — the burst demand in the first minutes — and with sustained high-intensity exercise. This is why patients can walk for hours but cannot sprint, and why the occupational history matters (a manual labourer has much more disability than a sedentary office worker). The degree of limitation depends on how often the patient encounters the intensity threshold at which glycogen would normally be needed.
💡 Key Insight 2
Why isometric exercise is particularly dangerous
Isometric exercise (contracting a muscle against a fixed resistance — pushing against a wall, holding a position, gripping tightly) is especially problematic in McArdle disease. During isometric exercise, the muscle compresses its own blood supply, preventing the delivery of blood glucose and fatty acids that would otherwise partially compensate. The muscle is simultaneously demanding maximum ATP (high isometric force) and cutting off its alternative fuel supply. This is why tasks like gripping firmly, pushing heavy objects without moving, and holding fixed positions rapidly trigger contractures in McArdle patients even before isotonic exercise of comparable effort.
💡 Key Insight 3
The AMP deaminase connection — what it tells you about the forearm test
The forearm test exploits two independent markers: lactate (glycolysis working?) and ammonia (patient actually exercised?). AMP deaminase deficiency is a separate myopathy where the purine nucleotide cycle is blocked — these patients have normal lactate rise but flat ammonia (the exact opposite of McArdle). This is an important differential. In McArdle: lactate flat, ammonia up. In AMP deaminase deficiency: lactate up (glycolysis works fine), ammonia flat. In inadequate exercise: both flat. Understanding why ammonia rises in McArdle (AMP → IMP + NH₃ from the energy crisis, not from glycolysis) is the key mechanistic point that exams test in R&A format.
⚖️ Comparison Trap 1
McArdle (GSD V) vs Hers Disease (GSD VI)
Both involve Glycogen Phosphorylase deficiency. But:

McArdle: Muscle phosphorylase deficient (PYGM). Normal fasting glucose. Exercise intolerance, cramps, myoglobinuria. No hepatomegaly. No lactic acidosis at rest. Forearm test: lactate flat.

Hers: Liver phosphorylase deficient (PYGL). Mild fasting hypoglycaemia. Hepatomegaly. No muscle symptoms. No myoglobinuria. Forearm test: normal (muscle phosphorylase intact). The isoenzyme difference (PYGM vs PYGL) is what separates them completely.

Exam pattern: If the question mentions hepatomegaly and mild hypoglycaemia with no muscle symptoms → think Hers. If it mentions exercise intolerance, cramps, dark urine, and normal fasting glucose → think McArdle.
⚖️ Comparison Trap 2
McArdle vs Pompe Disease (GSD II)
Both cause muscle glycogen accumulation. But the mechanism and presentation are completely different:

McArdle: Cytosolic glycogenolysis blocked (myophosphorylase absent). Glycogen accumulates in the cytoplasm. Exercise-induced symptoms. Normal between episodes. Forearm test abnormal. No cardiac involvement. CK elevated.

Pompe: Lysosomal acid α-glucosidase (GAA) absent — lysosomal glycogen degradation blocked. Glycogen accumulates in lysosomes in multiple tissues. Exercise-independent symptoms — progressive weakness regardless of activity. Infantile form: massive cardiomegaly and hypotonia → death by age 2. Adult form: progressive respiratory and limb-girdle weakness. Forearm test normal (cytosolic glycolysis intact). No myoglobinuria pattern.

The lysosomal vs cytosolic distinction is critical — Pompe affects the backup pathway for glycogen turnover; McArdle affects the main exercise-activated glycogenolysis pathway.
⚖️ Comparison Trap 3
McArdle vs Von Gierke — The Opposite Disease
McArdle and Von Gierke are conceptual mirror images — and examiners exploit this symmetry.

Von Gierke: Liver only affected. Presents in infancy. Fasting hypoglycaemia (severe). Lactic acidosis at rest. Hepatomegaly. Normal exercise capacity. Glucagon fails to raise glucose. G6Pase absent.

McArdle: Muscle only affected. Presents in childhood/early adulthood. Normal fasting glucose. Normal lactate at rest. No hepatomegaly. Exercise intolerance. Glucagon response completely normal. Myophosphorylase absent.

The symmetry exams love: In Von Gierke, lactate is high at rest and worsens with glucagon. In McArdle, lactate is normal at rest but fails to rise with exercise. In Von Gierke, the liver is the problem. In McArdle, the liver is fine. Examiners will swap features between these two diseases — always check which tissue is affected and what happens at rest vs exercise.
R&A Framework
How to Think Through McArdle R&A Questions

McArdle R&A questions fall into three categories: (1) mechanism of the second wind, (2) the forearm test results, and (3) why fasting glucose is normal.

Second wind R&A pattern: The assertion will usually be true (patients can exercise better after the second wind). The reason will describe the mechanism — the key is whether it correctly identifies oxidative metabolism taking over as the explanation, rather than incorrect reasons like "glycogen finally being mobilised" (wrong — myophosphorylase is still absent), or "lactic acid being cleared" (wrong — there was no lactic acid buildup because there was no glycolysis). If the reason says glycogen is now accessible during the second wind — that's false.

Forearm test R&A pattern: The assertion will state lactate fails to rise. The reason must correctly explain this via absence of myophosphorylase → no G6P from glycogen → no glycolytic substrate. Common wrong reasons: "glycolysis enzymes are absent" (false — glycolysis is intact, only its glycogen-derived substrate is absent), or "the muscle cannot take up glucose from the blood" (false — GLUT transporters are normal).

Normal fasting glucose R&A pattern: The assertion (fasting glucose is normal) is always true. The reason must cite liver phosphorylase (PYGL) being unaffected — not myophosphorylase. If a reason says "glycogen breakdown is completely unaffected in McArdle disease" — this is false (muscle glycogen breakdown IS affected). Only hepatic glycogen breakdown is unaffected.

Diagnosis
How McArdle Disease Is Diagnosed

Diagnosis of McArdle disease follows a stepwise approach. The clinical history is often strongly suggestive, and specific investigations confirm it. The challenge is that McArdle is underdiagnosed — many patients are told for years that they are simply unfit. A persistently elevated CK in a young person with exercise intolerance should always raise suspicion.

Diagnostic Pathway
1
Clinical History — The Pattern Is Diagnostic
Exercise intolerance from childhood or early adulthood. Symptoms specifically during exercise (not at rest). The "second wind" phenomenon — if present, this is virtually pathognomonic. Episodes of dark urine following intense exercise. Family history (autosomal recessive — siblings may be affected). Important to ask specifically: Can the patient exercise after a 10-minute warm-up better than at the start? Do symptoms resolve completely with rest? Has the patient ever had dark urine after exercise?
2
Serum CK — Baseline Elevation
A simple blood test — serum CK is elevated even at rest in McArdle disease, typically 3–10× normal. This is an important screening test. An unexplained elevated CK in a young person should prompt investigation for a muscle disease. CK does not specifically diagnose McArdle, but its elevation confirms myopathy and triggers further investigation. During exercise episodes, CK can be extremely high (>10,000 U/L), confirming rhabdomyolysis.
3
Non-Ischaemic Forearm Exercise Test
The key functional test. The patient exercises the forearm (hand gripping) for 1–2 minutes without blood flow occlusion (non-ischaemic version is now preferred). Venous blood sampled at baseline, 1, 3, 6, and 10 minutes for lactate and ammonia. In McArdle: lactate flat, ammonia rises ≥3×. Important: the ischaemic version can cause severe contracture or rhabdomyolysis in McArdle patients and is no longer recommended as the primary test. If the patient does not exercise adequately, both lactate and ammonia stay flat — a falsely negative result that must be interpreted cautiously.
4
Genetic Testing — Now the Gold Standard
Sequencing of the PYGM gene confirms the diagnosis. The most common mutation in McArdle disease is p.Arg50* (R50X) — a premature stop codon that results in complete absence of myophosphorylase. This mutation accounts for approximately 81% of affected alleles in European populations, making targeted mutation screening highly effective. Genetic testing has largely replaced muscle biopsy for diagnosis, as it is non-invasive and definitive. Family members can also be tested for carrier status.
5
Muscle Biopsy — When Genetic Testing Is Equivocal
Muscle biopsy with histochemistry showing absent myophosphorylase activity (using the histochemical stain for phosphorylase — affected fibres stain negative, while unaffected innervated blood vessels stain positive as an internal control) and PAS staining showing excess glycogen (subsarcolemmal deposits). Electron microscopy reveals characteristic glycogen deposits just below the sarcolemma. Biopsy is now reserved for cases where genetic testing is inconclusive or when additional information about the extent of muscle damage is needed.
Management
Living and Training with McArdle Disease

There is no cure for McArdle disease. Management is focused on preventing myoglobinuria and AKI, maximising exercise capacity, and improving quality of life. The good news is that with the right strategies, most patients can live active and fulfilling lives with substantially reduced symptom burden.

Exercise Strategy — The Most Important Intervention
Supervised aerobic exercise training is the most evidence-based intervention — it dramatically improves exercise capacity by increasing cardiovascular fitness and improving the delivery of blood glucose and fatty acids to muscle. The goal is to improve the alternative fuel systems. Warm-up protocol: always start very slowly for 10 minutes before any sustained activity to reach the second wind before intensifying. Avoid: sudden intense bursts without warm-up, isometric exercise, pushing through cramps. Use a heart rate monitor — staying within 60–70% of maximum heart rate minimises the risk of entering the danger zone.
Dietary Strategies
Pre-exercise carbohydrate ingestion: taking a glucose drink or sucrose snack 5 minutes before exercise raises blood glucose → more substrate available for the muscle → reduces symptoms during the early phase before the second wind. This is one of the most practically effective strategies. High-protein diet: controversial but some evidence suggests increased dietary protein may support gluconeogenesis and provide amino acid substrates during exercise. Avoid prolonged fasting before planned exercise — being in a post-absorptive state with low blood glucose worsens early exercise tolerance significantly.
Managing Acute Episodes — Myoglobinuria
Any episode of dark urine after exercise requires immediate medical attention. Treatment: aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (large volumes of isotonic saline — the goal is to maintain urine output at 200–300 mL/hour, diluting myoglobin in the tubules and flushing it out before it precipitates). Urine alkalinisation (sodium bicarbonate in IV fluids) reduces myoglobin precipitation (myoglobin is less soluble in acidic urine). Avoid NSAIDs and other nephrotoxic drugs. Monitor renal function, urine output, and CK levels closely. Haemodialysis if AKI develops and is severe.
Pharmacological & Emerging Therapies
No proven disease-modifying drug therapy currently exists for McArdle disease. Ribose supplementation (a pentose sugar that partially bypasses the glycolytic block) showed initial promise but has not been consistently effective in trials. Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) was proposed as a cofactor for Phosphorylase Kinase modulation but evidence is weak. Sucrose before exercise remains the most reliably effective pharmacological-adjacent strategy. Gene therapy (AAV-mediated PYGM delivery) is in early preclinical development. Creatine monohydrate supplementation is used by some patients to boost the PCr buffer — evidence is mixed but it may slightly extend the symptom-free early exercise phase.
For Your Exams
The Five Things You Must Know Cold

1. Myophosphorylase (PYGM) is absent. Liver phosphorylase (PYGL) is completely normal. Fasting blood glucose is normal. There is no hepatic involvement whatsoever.

2. The forearm exercise test shows flat lactate (no glycogenolysis → no glycolytic substrate) but rising ammonia (AMP deaminase → AMP → IMP + NH₃ is glycolysis-independent). This pattern is pathognomonic. Flat lactate + flat ammonia = patient didn't exercise, not McArdle.

3. The "second wind" occurs because cardiovascular adaptation increases blood flow to muscle during the early crisis, and when the patient slows down, fatty acid oxidation + blood glucose finally meets the reduced demand. Glycogen is NOT being mobilised during the second wind — myophosphorylase is still absent.

4. Myoglobinuria from sustained contracture is the most dangerous acute complication. Dark urine after exercise = emergency. Treatment is aggressive IV fluids and urine alkalinisation to prevent AKI.

5. CK is elevated at baseline (even at rest) and extremely elevated after exercise. This distinguishes McArdle from deconditioning (where CK is normal at rest) and makes it the primary screening test in a young person with exercise intolerance.

Which of the following correctly explains why fasting blood glucose is completely normal in McArdle disease?

Assertion
Myoglobinuria in McArdle disease is a medical emergency requiring aggressive IV fluid resuscitation.
Reason
Myoglobin precipitates in renal tubules in acidic concentrated urine, causing tubular obstruction and acute kidney injury.
Both correct. Myoglobin from necrotic muscle is filtered by the kidneys and precipitates in acidic concentrated urine, obstructing tubules and causing AKI. Aggressive IV fluids dilute myoglobin, increase urine flow, and with bicarbonate alkalinise the urine to reduce precipitation. This is a genuine medical emergency.
Assertion
McArdle disease patients have a completely normal response to glucagon administration.
Reason
Glucagon stimulates liver glycogen phosphorylase (PYGL), which is unaffected in McArdle disease, causing normal hepatic glycogenolysis and blood glucose rise.
Both correct. Because McArdle only affects PYGM (muscle isoform), liver glycogen phosphorylase (PYGL) responds normally to glucagon. Blood glucose rises normally. This is the key difference from Von Gierke where glucagon causes lactic acidosis without blood glucose rise.
Assertion
In the forearm exercise test, a patient who fails to exercise adequately will show the same result as a McArdle patient.
Reason
Both inadequate exercise and McArdle disease result in failure of venous lactate to rise after forearm exercise.
The assertion is partially correct - inadequate exercise does produce flat lactate. But the reason is incomplete. In McArdle disease, ammonia rises normally (AMP deaminase activated by energy crisis). In inadequate exercise, BOTH lactate AND ammonia stay flat. This ammonia difference is diagnostic. Reason does not fully explain - hence B.
Assertion
Isometric exercise is particularly dangerous for McArdle patients compared to isotonic exercise.
Reason
During isometric contraction the muscle compresses its own blood supply, preventing delivery of circulating glucose and fatty acids that would partially compensate for absent glycogenolysis.
Both correct. In isotonic exercise blood flows freely to working muscle. In isometric exercise, sustained contraction compresses intramuscular vessels, cutting off delivery of blood glucose and fatty acids. Since McArdle patients rely entirely on these alternative fuels, cutting off delivery causes rapid energy failure and contracture.
Assertion
The gene responsible for McArdle disease is located on the X chromosome.
Reason
McArdle disease shows a sex-linked inheritance pattern with males more commonly affected.
Both false. McArdle disease is caused by PYGM mutations on chromosome 11q13 - autosomal, not X-linked. Inheritance is autosomal recessive. Males and females are equally affected. X-linked recessive with male predominance applies to G6PD deficiency, not McArdle.
