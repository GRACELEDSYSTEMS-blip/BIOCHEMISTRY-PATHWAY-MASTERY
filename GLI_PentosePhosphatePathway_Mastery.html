Pentose Phosphate Pathway Mastery
A parallel pathway to glycolysis running in the cytosol. Its primary outputs are NADPH for reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defence, and ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis — not ATP.

Cytosol of all cells. Most active in tissues with high biosynthetic or antioxidant demands: liver (fatty acid + steroid synthesis), adipose tissue, adrenal cortex (steroid synthesis), red blood cells (antioxidant defence), rapidly dividing cells (nucleotide synthesis), and phagocytes (respiratory burst).
Entry Point & Relationship to Glycolysis
Glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) is the entry substrate — the same G6P produced by Hexokinase in step 1 of glycolysis. The cell decides whether G6P goes into glycolysis or the PPP based on its current needs. This is not an either/or decision permanently — intermediates of the non-oxidative phase can re-enter glycolysis.
Two Phases — Different Purposes
Oxidative phase (irreversible): produces NADPH and ribulose-5-phosphate. Regulated by NADPH:NADP⁺ ratio. Non-oxidative phase (reversible): interconverts sugar phosphates to produce ribose-5-phosphate or recycle back to glycolysis depending on the cell's needs.
Overall Equation (oxidative phase, per G6P)
G6P + 2 NADP⁺ + H₂O
→ Ribulose-5-phosphate + 2 NADPH + 2 H⁺ + CO₂
⚡ NADPH — Why This Coenzyme Is Critical
Antioxidant Defence
NADPH reduces oxidised glutathione (GSSG) back to reduced glutathione (GSH) via Glutathione Reductase. GSH neutralises reactive oxygen species (H₂O₂, peroxides). RBCs depend entirely on PPP-derived NADPH for this — they have no mitochondria to provide alternative reducing power. Without it, oxidative stress destroys RBC membranes.
Fatty Acid Synthesis
Every 2-carbon addition in fatty acid elongation requires 2 NADPH. The liver, adipose tissue, and mammary glands are heavy consumers of NADPH for lipogenesis. Fatty acid synthase complex uses NADPH to reduce the growing chain. This is why tissues doing active fat synthesis have high PPP activity.
Steroid & Cholesterol Synthesis
Steroid hormone biosynthesis (in adrenal cortex, gonads) and cholesterol synthesis both require NADPH. The adrenal cortex has exceptionally high PPP activity specifically to supply NADPH for cortisol, aldosterone, and sex hormone synthesis via cytochrome P450 hydroxylation reactions.
Respiratory Burst (Phagocytes)
NADPH Oxidase in neutrophils and macrophages uses NADPH to reduce O₂ to superoxide (O₂⁻), which then generates H₂O₂, HOCl, and other reactive species that kill ingested bacteria. This is the respiratory burst. Chronic Granulomatous Disease is caused by a defective NADPH Oxidase — patients cannot kill certain bacteria despite being able to phagocytose them.
Cytochrome P450 Reactions
Hepatic P450 enzymes that hydroxylate drugs, xenobiotics, and bile acid precursors all require NADPH as the electron donor. This is why high drug metabolism activity in the liver correlates with high PPP activity — the liver needs a continuous NADPH supply to process foreign compounds.
Nitric Oxide Synthesis
Nitric Oxide Synthase (NOS) requires NADPH to produce NO from arginine. NO is a critical signalling molecule for vascular tone (vasodilation), neurotransmission, and immune function. All three NOS isoforms (eNOS, nNOS, iNOS) are NADPH-dependent.

Explanation
G6PD catalyses the first oxidation of G6P, transferring electrons to NADP⁺ to produce NADPH and the lactone 6-phosphogluconolactone. The product immediately undergoes spontaneous (and enzyme-catalysed) hydrolysis in step 2.

The regulatory logic is beautifully simple: when NADPH is abundant, NADPH inhibits G6PD, slowing the pathway. When NADPH is consumed (in antioxidant defence, fatty acid synthesis, or any other NADPH-requiring reaction), NADP⁺ rises, relieves the inhibition, and G6PD speeds up. The pathway self-regulates to match demand — the cell never produces more NADPH than it needs, and it never runs short when NADPH is being consumed.

This is why oxidative stress — such as from an oxidant drug or infection — dramatically increases PPP flux in RBCs: the oxidative stress consumes NADPH (via glutathione reductase), NADP⁺ rises, G6PD is activated, and NADPH production surges to meet the demand. In G6PD-deficient individuals, this compensatory surge cannot occur.

Inter-pathway link: GlycolysisG6P is the substrate shared between glycolysis and the PPP. G6PD competes with Phosphoglucose Isomerase (Step 2 of glycolysis) for G6P. The NADPH:NADP⁺ ratio determines which enzyme predominates. Glutathione SystemNADPH produced here reduces GSSG → 2 GSH via Glutathione Reductase. GSH then reduces H₂O₂ to H₂O via Glutathione Peroxidase. The entire antioxidant system in RBCs depends on this NADPH supply.

Explanation
A hydrolysis reaction — the lactone ring of 6-phosphogluconolactone is opened by the addition of water to produce the open-chain 6-phosphogluconate. This reaction occurs spontaneously even without the enzyme, but the enzyme dramatically speeds it up to ensure the reaction doesn't stall. No energy is generated or consumed.

The main purpose of this step is simply to convert the lactone to a form that is a suitable substrate for the next oxidative decarboxylation. Without step 2, the lactone could accumulate and inhibit G6PD via product inhibition, slowing the entire pathway.

Explanation
An oxidative decarboxylation step — simultaneously oxidising 6-phosphogluconate (electrons to NADP⁺) and decarboxylating it (releasing CO₂), producing the 5-carbon ribulose-5-phosphate. This is the second and final NADPH produced in the oxidative phase, and it marks the end of the irreversible portion of the pathway.

Ribulose-5-phosphate is the branch point of the non-oxidative phase — it can be isomerised to ribose-5-phosphate (for nucleotide synthesis) or epimerised to xylulose-5-phosphate (to enter the transketolase/transaldolase reactions). Which direction it takes depends on what the cell currently needs.

This step uses NADP⁺ specifically, not NAD⁺ — an important exam distinction. NADPH (not NADH) is the reducing agent for anabolic reactions and antioxidant defence. The choice of NADP⁺ as the electron acceptor here is what makes the PPP's output useful for biosynthesis, rather than for the ETC (which processes NADH).

Non-Oxidative Phase — Carbon Shuffling
Transketolase + Transaldolase · Ribulose-5-P ⇌ Glycolytic intermediates
Reversible ▼
The non-oxidative phase does not produce NADPH — its job is to interconvert sugar phosphates of different carbon lengths, routing them toward whatever the cell needs most: ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis, or fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate to re-enter glycolysis. Two enzymes do all the work.
Transketolase — transfers 2-carbon units
Transketolase transfers a 2-carbon fragment (called a glycolaldehyde unit) from a ketose donor to an aldose acceptor. It requires Thiamine Pyrophosphate (TPP) as its cofactor — the same TPP required by PDC and α-KG Dehydrogenase Complex. This is clinically important: thiamine deficiency impairs all three enzymes simultaneously, and measuring RBC Transketolase activity is a functional test of thiamine status used in clinical settings.

Key reactions catalysed by Transketolase:
· Xylulose-5-P (5C) + Ribose-5-P (5C) → Glyceraldehyde-3-P (3C) + Sedoheptulose-7-P (7C)
· Xylulose-5-P (5C) + Erythrose-4-P (4C) → Glyceraldehyde-3-P (3C) + Fructose-6-P (6C)
Transaldolase — transfers 3-carbon units
Transaldolase transfers a 3-carbon dihydroxyacetone unit from a ketose to an aldose. It does not require TPP or any other cofactor — it forms a Schiff base intermediate with a lysine residue in its active site.

Key reaction catalysed by Transaldolase:
· Sedoheptulose-7-P (7C) + Glyceraldehyde-3-P (3C) → Erythrose-4-P (4C) + Fructose-6-P (6C)
Carbon flow — when NADPH is needed, ribose recycled back to glycolysis:
3 × Ribulose-5-P (5C = 15C total)
↓ Transketolase + Transaldolase
2 × Fructose-6-P (6C) + 1 × Glyceraldehyde-3-P (3C) = 15C total
↓ Re-enter glycolysis
Net: 3 G6P → 6 NADPH + 3 CO₂ + 2 F6P + 1 G3P (all carbon recycled)
The key insight: The non-oxidative phase gives the cell metabolic flexibility. If ribose-5-phosphate is needed urgently (during rapid DNA replication), glycolytic intermediates (F6P and G3P) can run through the non-oxidative phase in reverse to generate ribose-5-phosphate without producing NADPH. If NADPH is the priority, the oxidative phase runs and the resulting ribose-5-phosphate is recycled back through the non-oxidative phase into glycolysis — effectively trading G6P for NADPH while returning all the carbon to the glycolytic pool.

Inter-pathway link: GlycolysisF6P and G3P produced in the non-oxidative phase directly re-enter glycolysis at steps 3 and 6 respectively. This connects the PPP's carbon recycling to glycolysis, ensuring no carbon is wasted. Nucleotide SynthesisRibose-5-phosphate is the direct precursor for purine and pyrimidine nucleotide synthesis (via PRPP — phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate). Both DNA replication and RNA synthesis depend on this supply. Aromatic Amino AcidsErythrose-4-phosphate (an intermediate of the non-oxidative phase) is a precursor for synthesis of aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan) in plants and bacteria via the shikimate pathway — though not in humans. High-yield for understanding why humans must obtain these from diet.
Regulatory Overview
The PPP is regulated almost exclusively at G6PD — the first and rate-limiting step. The primary regulatory signal is the NADPH:NADP⁺ ratio: high NADPH inhibits G6PD; low NADPH (high NADP⁺) activates it. This simple feedback loop ensures the pathway's output matches demand with remarkable precision — the cell produces exactly as much NADPH as it is consuming, no more and no less. Hormonal regulation (insulin) adjusts the baseline level of G6PD expression for longer-term changes in biosynthetic demand.

Rate-Limiting

Oxidative stress — indirectly: ROS consume NADPH via glutathione reductase, raising NADP⁺.
The ratio-based control: The NADPH:NADP⁺ ratio in most cells is approximately 100:1 under normal conditions — far in favour of NADPH. This means G6PD is nearly completely inhibited at rest. When any NADPH-consuming process activates (fatty acid synthesis, oxidative stress response, steroid synthesis), the ratio falls, G6PD is relieved of inhibition, and the pathway accelerates. The speed of this response is matched to the speed of NADPH consumption — instant feedback, no signalling cascade required.

Insulin's role: In the fed state, insulin promotes lipogenesis (fatty acid synthesis) in liver and adipose tissue, which requires large amounts of NADPH. Insulin simultaneously upregulates G6PD expression, ensuring the PPP has sufficient capacity to meet the increased NADPH demand for lipogenesis. This is why G6PD activity is high in the well-fed liver.
How the Three Modes Relate to Regulation
Mode 1 — NADPH only (e.g. RBCs, adrenal cortex)
NADP⁺ is continuously high (NADPH is being consumed). G6PD runs at full speed. Ribose-5-phosphate produced in the oxidative phase is recycled back through the non-oxidative phase to F6P and G3P, re-entering glycolysis. G6PD activity = high; non-oxidative phase runs in the recycling direction.
Mode 2 — Ribose-5-P only (e.g. dividing cells, bone marrow)
NADPH:NADP⁺ ratio is high — NADPH is not being heavily consumed. G6PD is inhibited. Ribose-5-phosphate is generated by running the non-oxidative phase in reverse, using F6P and G3P from glycolysis directly. G6PD activity = low; oxidative phase bypassed entirely.
Mode 3 — Both needed (e.g. liver, rapidly growing tumours)
NADP⁺ is moderately elevated (NADPH being consumed for lipogenesis), and ribose is needed for nucleotide synthesis. Oxidative phase runs to produce NADPH, and ribose-5-phosphate is used directly rather than recycled. G6PD moderately active; both outputs consumed.
Transketolase — Thiamine Dependence
Non-oxidative phase · TPP-dependent · Functionally regulated by thiamine availability
TPP Required
Transketolase is not regulated allosterically — its activity is determined primarily by Thiamine Pyrophosphate (TPP) availability. When thiamine is deficient, Transketolase activity falls, the non-oxidative phase stalls, and ribose-5-phosphate cannot be efficiently recycled into glycolysis or produced for nucleotide synthesis.

Clinical utility: Measuring Transketolase activity in red blood cells, and comparing it before and after addition of TPP in vitro (the "TPP effect"), is a sensitive functional test of thiamine status. A large increase in activity after TPP addition confirms thiamine deficiency — the enzyme was substrate-limited. This test detects functional deficiency even before overt clinical symptoms appear.
⚕️ Clinical correlations follow the biochemical chain — which enzyme or cofactor fails, what NADPH-dependent process collapses, and why that produces the specific presentation.
G6PD Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: Glucose-6-phosphate Dehydrogenase (Step 1 of PPP)
X-linked RecessiveMost Common Enzyme Deficiency Worldwide~400 million affected

Start from what G6PD does: it is the sole source of NADPH in red blood cells — RBCs have no mitochondria, no alternative NADPH-generating pathway. NADPH in RBCs exists for one primary purpose: maintaining GSH (reduced glutathione) via Glutathione Reductase, which neutralises H₂O₂ and other reactive oxygen species that constantly form during normal haemoglobin-carrying function.

In G6PD deficiency, NADPH production is impaired. Under basal conditions, the residual G6PD activity is often sufficient and patients are asymptomatic. But when oxidative stress is imposed — by certain drugs, infections, or dietary triggers — the demand for NADPH surges beyond what the deficient enzyme can supply. GSH falls, GSSG accumulates, ROS attack the RBC membrane and haemoglobin. Denatured haemoglobin forms Heinz bodies visible on special staining. The spleen recognises these damaged cells and removes them — producing an episode of acute haemolytic anaemia.

Triggers to know: antimalarials (primaquine, chloroquine), antibiotics (dapsone, nitrofurantoin, sulphonamides), fava beans (divicine and isouramil — oxidant compounds in broad beans, causing favism), naphthalene (mothballs), and infections (fever + oxidative stress together).

Why it's so common: G6PD deficiency is prevalent in malaria-endemic regions (Africa, Mediterranean, Middle East, Southeast Asia) because heterozygous females and hemizygous males have some protection against Plasmodium falciparum malaria — the parasite is more vulnerable to oxidative stress in G6PD-deficient RBCs, giving carriers a survival advantage. This is the same selective pressure that maintains sickle cell trait.

Neonatal jaundice: G6PD-deficient neonates are at high risk of severe neonatal jaundice, because neonatal RBCs are particularly susceptible to haemolysis. This is a preventable cause of kernicterus (brain damage from bilirubin) in endemic regions.
Favism
Mechanism: Oxidant compounds in fava beans → NADPH depletion in G6PD-deficient RBCs
Dietary TriggerG6PD-related

Favism is acute haemolytic anaemia triggered specifically by ingestion of fava beans (broad beans, Vicia faba) in G6PD-deficient individuals. The active oxidants are divicine and isouramil — compounds derived from the vicine and convicine in fava beans. These undergo redox cycling in RBCs, generating large quantities of H₂O₂ and superoxide. In a G6PD-sufficient person, the surge in NADPH production via the PPP neutralises these oxidants rapidly. In a G6PD-deficient individual, the NADPH response is inadequate, GSH falls rapidly, and acute haemolysis follows within hours of ingestion.

Favism was the first recognised form of G6PD deficiency — the ancient Mediterranean prohibition on eating fava beans (attributed to Pythagoras) likely reflected empirical observation of this reaction in a G6PD-deficient population. The haemolytic severity can vary by G6PD mutation type — Class I mutations (severely deficient) cause the most severe favism.
Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD)
Mechanism: NADPH Oxidase deficiency → failure of respiratory burst in phagocytes
X-linked (most common) / Autosomal RecessivePrimary Immunodeficiency

NADPH Oxidase in neutrophils and macrophages uses NADPH (sourced from the PPP) to reduce O₂ to superoxide (O₂⁻): NADPH + 2O₂ → NADP⁺ + 2O₂⁻ + H⁺. Superoxide is then converted to H₂O₂, HOCl (via myeloperoxidase), and other reactive species that kill ingested bacteria and fungi — the respiratory burst.

In CGD, NADPH Oxidase itself is deficient (most commonly the gp91phox subunit, X-linked). Phagocytes can engulf bacteria normally but cannot kill catalase-positive organisms (Staphylococcus aureus, Aspergillus, Serratia, Nocardia, Burkholderia) because these organisms produce their own catalase, which destroys the small amounts of H₂O₂ that the cell might otherwise generate via alternative pathways.

Clinically: recurrent life-threatening bacterial and fungal infections beginning in childhood, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and the characteristic granuloma formation (the body's attempt to wall off organisms it cannot kill). Dihydrorhodamine flow cytometry (DHR test) confirms the diagnosis — G6PD-deficient phagocytes fail to oxidise DHR, whereas normal phagocytes do during the respiratory burst.
Thiamine Deficiency — Transketolase Dysfunction
Affected enzyme: Transketolase (non-oxidative phase) — requires TPP
NutritionalMulti-Enzyme Impact

While Wernicke's encephalopathy and beriberi (covered in the Krebs Cycle Mastery file) result from PDC and α-KG Dehydrogenase Complex failure, thiamine deficiency also impairs Transketolase in the non-oxidative phase of the PPP. This has two consequences: ribose-5-phosphate cannot be efficiently recycled through the non-oxidative phase, impairing nucleotide synthesis; and the PPP's connection back to glycolysis is disrupted.

Practically, Transketolase impairment is used clinically as the most sensitive early indicator of thiamine deficiency: RBC Transketolase activity is measured before and after addition of TPP in vitro. An increase in activity of more than 25% after TPP addition (the "TPP effect") indicates functional thiamine deficiency — the enzyme was substrate-limited by TPP availability, even if serum thiamine is not yet critically low. This test can detect deficiency before Wernicke's or beriberi develop, enabling earlier intervention.
