Clinical Features
What Is This Disease?
A Locked Door in the Liver

Imagine the liver as a warehouse that stores glucose as glycogen — a compact, branched molecule that can be rapidly unpacked when the body needs fuel. Every few hours between meals, this warehouse unpacks some glycogen and ships glucose out into the bloodstream to keep your brain, muscles, and organs running.

For glucose to be shipped out, it has to go through a specific process: glycogen breaks down into individual glucose units, each one gets tagged with a phosphate group to keep it inside the cell, and then — right at the very last step — an enzyme called Glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) removes that phosphate tag and opens the door. Free glucose walks out of the liver cell, enters the blood, and the rest of the body gets fed.

In Von Gierke disease, that enzyme — G6Pase — is missing. The door is locked. The liver can unpack glycogen perfectly. It can even make glucose through other pathways. But it cannot take that final step of removing the phosphate and releasing free glucose into the blood. Glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) builds up inside liver cells with nowhere to go.

🏭 The Warehouse Analogy
Think of G6P as packages that have been wrapped and labelled but never loaded onto the delivery truck — because the loading dock (G6Pase) is broken. The warehouse fills up with packages (G6P → glycogen overflow → hepatomegaly), the packers keep wrapping more (gluconeogenesis still running), but nothing ever ships out. The customers (brain, muscles) are starving even though the warehouse is overflowing. That's Von Gierke disease.

This single enzyme deficiency creates a domino effect — a cascade of biochemical consequences that explains every single feature of this disease. The hypoglycaemia, the swollen liver, the high triglycerides, the lactic acidosis, the gout — every one of them traces back to that one broken enzyme at the end of the pathway.

The Core Concept
Why Only the Liver?

You might wonder — if G6Pase is broken, why doesn't every organ in the body get sick? The answer is simple: only the liver (and the kidney cortex) actually has Glucose-6-phosphatase in the first place.

Muscle cells, brain cells, heart cells — none of them have G6Pase. When those cells break down glycogen, the G6P they produce stays inside and feeds directly into their own glycolysis for energy. They were never designed to release glucose into the blood. That's the liver's exclusive job.

So Von Gierke disease is fundamentally a disease of hepatic glucose export failure. The liver is doing everything right up to the last step — it's breaking down glycogen, it's running gluconeogenesis — but it cannot export the final product. The consequence is that the rest of the body is chronically starved of glucose, while the liver is simultaneously drowning in it.

🔑
The single most important concept in Von Gierke disease: G6Pase is the final step shared by BOTH glycogenolysis AND gluconeogenesis. Blocking it doesn't just impair one pathway — it blocks the liver's entire glucose-releasing capacity from every source simultaneously. This is why the disease is so severe.
Where It Fits in the Pathway
The Last Step of Everything

In normal metabolism, glucose-6-phosphate sits at a crossroads. It can go into glycolysis (broken down for energy), into the pentose phosphate pathway (for NADPH and ribose production), into glycogen synthesis (stored as glycogen), or — in the liver only — it can be dephosphorylated by G6Pase to become free glucose for export.

In Von Gierke disease, that last exit route is sealed. G6P cannot leave as free glucose. So it backs up and floods every other direction instead:

Into glycolysis → excess pyruvate → excess lactate → lactic acidosis

Into glycogen synthesis → glycogen accumulates in the liver → hepatomegaly

Into lipid synthesis → excess Acetyl-CoA → fatty acids and triglycerides → hypertriglyceridaemia and fatty liver

Cannot exit as glucose → blood glucose falls → severe fasting hypoglycaemia

Plain English Summary
G6P is like water building up behind a blocked drain. It doesn't just sit there — it overflows in every direction available. Each overflow direction produces a different clinical feature. Understanding the disease is really just understanding where the overflow goes.
Step-by-Step Mechanism
From One Missing Enzyme to Six Clinical Problems

Every clinical feature of Von Gierke disease follows directly from the accumulation of Glucose-6-phosphate. Let's trace the chain step by step — starting from the broken enzyme and working outward to each consequence.

The Primary Block
1
Missing Enzyme
Glucose-6-phosphatase is absent from the ER membrane
G6Pase sits on the inner membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) — not the cytosol. This is important: G6P has to be transported into the ER lumen by a transporter (G6PT, encoded by the SLC37A4 gene) before G6Pase can act on it. In GSD Type Ia, G6Pase itself is missing. In GSD Type Ib, the transporter is missing — same clinical picture, different gene. The enzyme normally removes the phosphate from G6P, producing free glucose and inorganic phosphate. Without it, G6P accumulates in the cytosol.
G6PC gene mutation → no functional G6Pase protein → G6P cannot be dephosphorylated
2
Primary Accumulation
Glucose-6-phosphate builds up in every hepatocyte
G6P is produced by two processes running constantly in the liver: glycogenolysis (glycogen → G1P → G6P via Phosphoglucomutase) and gluconeogenesis (pyruvate/lactate/amino acids → ... → G6P). Both pipelines keep feeding G6P into the hepatocyte. Normally, G6Pase drains it out as free glucose. Without that drain, G6P accumulates continuously — especially during fasting when both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis are running at maximum speed.
Glycogenolysis + Gluconeogenesis → G6P → nowhere to go → intracellular accumulation
3
Immediate Consequence
Blood glucose cannot be maintained during fasting
The liver's job during fasting is to release glucose into the blood to keep blood glucose at ~4–5 mmol/L. With G6Pase missing, this is impossible — G6P is trapped inside hepatocytes regardless of how much glycogen is broken down or how hard gluconeogenesis runs. Blood glucose falls within 2–4 hours of fasting (a very short window — most healthy adults can fast for 12–14 hours without significant hypoglycaemia). The brain, which depends almost entirely on glucose, is particularly vulnerable.
The G6P Overflow — Where It Goes and What It Causes
A
Overflow Route 1 → Glycogen Synthesis
G6P → Glycogen → Hepatomegaly
G6P can be converted to Glucose-1-phosphate (by Phosphoglucomutase running in reverse) and then to UDP-Glucose, which Glycogen Synthase incorporates into glycogen. The liver cells literally fill up with glycogen — the organ swells enormously. Hepatomegaly in Von Gierke can be dramatic: the liver may reach the iliac crests, making the abdomen visibly protuberant even in infancy. The liver is not inflamed or fibrotic initially — it is simply packed with glycogen. Over years, continued glycogen accumulation and the toxic effects of the metabolic derangements can lead to hepatic complications including adenoma formation.
G6P → G1P → UDP-Glucose → Glycogen accumulation → hepatomegaly
B
Overflow Route 2 → Glycolysis
G6P → Glycolysis → Pyruvate → Lactate → Lactic Acidosis
G6P entering glycolysis produces excess pyruvate. But pyruvate cannot be fully processed by the TCA cycle when it's being produced in such large quantities — and crucially, the liver is simultaneously trying to use pyruvate as a gluconeogenic substrate, but that glucose cannot be exported either. The result: pyruvate is shunted to lactate by Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH), which regenerates NAD⁺ to keep glycolysis running. Lactate accumulates in the blood — sometimes reaching 5–10× normal levels (normal: 0.5–2 mmol/L; Von Gierke: may exceed 10 mmol/L). This lactic acidosis can be the most life-threatening metabolic abnormality in a crisis, producing a high anion gap metabolic acidosis.
G6P → Fructose-6-P → ... → Pyruvate → Lactate (LDH) → elevated blood lactate → metabolic acidosis
C
Overflow Route 3 → Lipid Synthesis
G6P → Pyruvate → Acetyl-CoA → Triglycerides → Hyperlipidaemia
The excess pyruvate that isn't converted to lactate enters the mitochondria and becomes Acetyl-CoA via Pyruvate Dehydrogenase. Acetyl-CoA is the building block of fatty acids. With an overabundance of Acetyl-CoA and nowhere productive to put it, the liver switches to de novo lipogenesis — making fatty acids at high rate. These are packaged into VLDL (very low density lipoproteins) and released into the blood, causing hypertriglyceridaemia. Simultaneously, G6P can enter the pentose phosphate pathway, generating NADPH to fuel even more lipid synthesis. The excess fat also deposits in the liver itself, contributing to fatty liver (hepatic steatosis). Cholesterol synthesis also increases via the same Acetyl-CoA overflow. Clinically, patients may develop xanthomas (fatty deposits in skin) and pancreatitis from extremely high triglycerides.
Acetyl-CoA → Malonyl-CoA → Palmitate → Triglycerides → VLDL → hypertriglyceridaemia
D
Secondary Consequence → Uric Acid
G6P Overflow → Pentose Phosphate Pathway → Purine → Uric Acid → Hyperuricaemia
Some of the excess G6P enters the Pentose Phosphate Pathway (PPP), generating ribose-5-phosphate. Ribose-5-phosphate is the backbone of purines (adenine, guanine). With excess ribose available, purine synthesis accelerates, and purine degradation increases accordingly — producing more uric acid. Additionally, the G6P-driven increase in glycolysis consumes ATP rapidly, raising AMP. AMP is degraded through the purine nucleotide cycle, also generating uric acid. Compounding this, the high lactate levels compete with uric acid for renal tubular secretion — meaning the kidney excretes less uric acid even as production rises. The result: hyperuricaemia, which if untreated can cause gout and kidney stones. Children with Von Gierke typically don't develop gout (uric acid needs years to crystallise), but adolescents and adults with poorly controlled disease do.
PPP ↑ → ribose-5-P ↑ → purine synthesis ↑ → uric acid ↑; lactate competes with urate for renal secretion → hyperuricaemia
E
Secondary Consequence → Kidney
G6P Accumulation in Kidney → Nephromegaly → Long-term Renal Disease
The kidney cortex also expresses G6Pase and is therefore also affected. Glycogen and fat accumulate in renal tubular cells, causing nephromegaly (enlarged kidneys). Long-term, glycogen accumulation in the glomeruli and tubules causes progressive renal dysfunction — focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) leading to proteinuria, and eventually chronic kidney disease in poorly controlled patients. The combination of hyperuricaemia and renal disease can be particularly damaging. Early patients who survived into adulthood before modern management frequently developed end-stage renal disease.
Critical Mechanism
Why Glucagon Cannot Help — The Diagnostic Paradox

In a healthy person, when blood glucose falls, the pancreas releases glucagon. Glucagon signals the liver to do two things: break down glycogen (glycogenolysis) and make new glucose (gluconeogenesis). Both processes produce G6P, which G6Pase then converts to free glucose for export. Blood glucose rises.

In Von Gierke disease, glucagon is released normally, and the liver responds normally — glycogenolysis accelerates, gluconeogenesis runs at maximum rate, G6P pours into the hepatocyte. But then nothing happens. G6P has nowhere to go. Giving glucagon to a Von Gierke patient doesn't raise blood glucose — it just makes G6P accumulate faster, worsening lactic acidosis.

🚰 The Plumbing Analogy
Glucagon is like turning up the water pressure in a pipe that has a blocked outlet. You're pushing harder, but nothing is getting through. The pressure just builds up, and water starts leaking out of every other crack — that's the worsening lactic acidosis and hypertriglyceridaemia when glucagon is given. The fix isn't more pressure — it's unblocking the drain (or bypassing it entirely with dietary management).

This is one of the key diagnostic tests for Von Gierke disease: the glucagon stimulation test. A dose of glucagon is given after a fast. In a normal child, blood glucose rises by at least 1.5–2 mmol/L. In a Von Gierke patient, blood glucose barely moves — but blood lactate rises significantly. The glucagon is stimulating glycogenolysis, G6P accumulates, and the overflow goes to lactate instead of glucose.

Technical Detail
Glucagon stimulation test findings in Von Gierke Type Ia:
— Blood glucose: minimal or no rise (<1 mmol/L increase)
— Blood lactate: rises significantly (paradoxical lactate increase)
— This pattern distinguishes Von Gierke from other causes of fasting hypoglycaemia
Clinical Presentation
How Von Gierke Presents — And Why

Von Gierke disease typically presents in the first few months of life, often when a breastfed infant begins sleeping longer between feeds — even a 3–4 hour gap can trigger hypoglycaemia. Alternatively, it presents when formula-fed infants are weaned or when feeding intervals increase. The initial presentation is usually a hypoglycaemic seizure or an incidental finding of an enormous liver during a routine examination.

Every clinical feature can be explained by the biochemistry — let's go through each one.

⚡
Severe Fasting Hypoglycaemia
Most dangerous feature · Presents earliest
Blood glucose drops to critically low levels (often <2 mmol/L, sometimes <1 mmol/L) within 2–4 hours of fasting. The brain runs almost exclusively on glucose — without it, neurons stop firing properly, producing irritability, lethargy, seizures, and if prolonged, brain damage. Unlike some other hypoglycaemic conditions, the hypoglycaemia in Von Gierke is not from insulin excess — insulin levels are actually low (the pancreas correctly senses low glucose and reduces insulin). The problem is purely hepatic: the liver cannot export glucose.
G6Pase absent → G6P cannot → free glucose → blood glucose ↓ → brain symptoms
🫀
Massive Hepatomegaly
Most visible feature · Present from infancy
The liver can become enormously enlarged — sometimes extending to the pelvis. The abdomen appears protuberant and the liver is firm on palpation. Initially this is due to glycogen and fat accumulation (not inflammation), so liver enzymes may be normal or only mildly elevated early on. Over years, hepatic adenomas can develop (benign tumours in the glycogen-laden liver), and there is a small but real risk of hepatocellular carcinoma if adenomas are present. The kidneys are also enlarged (nephromegaly) from G6Pase deficiency in renal cortex.
G6P → glycogen synthesis ↑ → hepatocytes packed with glycogen → organ enlargement
🧪
Lactic Acidosis
High anion gap · Life-threatening in crises
Blood lactate is chronically elevated (often 3–10 mmol/L; normal <2 mmol/L) because excess G6P floods glycolysis, producing excess pyruvate that is converted to lactate. This creates a high anion gap metabolic acidosis. During illness, surgery, or prolonged fasting — when glycogenolysis accelerates — lactate can spike dramatically and become life-threatening. Patients are also prone to lactic acidosis crises during intercurrent infections. Chronic lactic acidosis contributes to the hyperuricaemia (lactate competes with urate for renal tubular secretion).
G6P ↑ → glycolysis ↑ → pyruvate ↑ → lactate ↑ → metabolic acidosis
🩸
Hypertriglyceridaemia & Hypercholesterolaemia
Leads to xanthomas · Risk of pancreatitis
Triglycerides can be spectacularly elevated — sometimes exceeding 10–20 mmol/L (normal <1.7 mmol/L). This is because excess Acetyl-CoA from glycolysis drives massive de novo lipogenesis in the liver, and the resulting triglycerides are packaged into VLDL and released into the blood. The skin may show xanthomas (yellowish fatty deposits, particularly over knees and elbows). Extreme hypertriglyceridaemia raises the risk of acute pancreatitis. Cholesterol is also elevated. The lipid panel looks similar to familial hyperlipidaemia but is entirely secondary to the metabolic block.
Acetyl-CoA ↑ → fatty acid synthesis ↑ → VLDL ↑ → triglycerides ↑ in blood
💎
Hyperuricaemia & Gout
In older patients · Compounded by lactate
Uric acid is elevated for two reasons: increased production (PPP generates ribose-5-phosphate → purine synthesis → uric acid; ATP depletion from rapid glycolysis → AMP degradation → uric acid) and decreased excretion (lactate competes with urate for renal tubular secretion via OAT transporters). Children rarely develop gout (crystals take years to form), but adolescents and adults with poorly controlled disease develop tophi, gouty arthritis, and uric acid kidney stones. Managing the underlying metabolic disease dramatically reduces uric acid levels.
PPP ↑ → purines ↑ → uric acid ↑; lactate competes for renal urate excretion → hyperuricaemia
📏
Growth Retardation & Doll-Like Facies
Classic appearance · Improves with treatment
Chronic hypoglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, and poor nutrition impair growth hormone action and IGF-1 production, causing short stature. The combination of a protuberant abdomen (massive hepatomegaly), relatively short limbs, and a rounded, chubby-cheeked face produces the characteristic "doll-like" appearance described in textbooks. Fat accumulates subcutaneously (from excess lipid synthesis) and in the cheeks, giving a well-nourished appearance despite the metabolic chaos underneath. With proper dietary management started early, growth can be normalised.
Chronic metabolic stress → IGF-1 ↓ → growth retardation; fat accumulation → doll-like facies
🩺
Bleeding Tendency
Platelet dysfunction · Prolonged bleeding time
Patients with Von Gierke disease have a prolonged bleeding time despite a normal platelet count and normal coagulation factors. The reason is platelet dysfunction — platelets in Von Gierke disease have impaired aggregation and adhesion, particularly reduced release of ADP and impaired thromboxane synthesis. The exact mechanism is not fully elucidated but appears related to abnormal platelet energy metabolism from the underlying glycogen storage defect. This is clinically important before any surgical procedures — patients need specific haemostatic management.
Platelet glycogen storage defect → impaired platelet activation → prolonged bleeding time
🫘
Renal Complications
Long-term · Progressive with age
The kidney cortex expresses G6Pase, so it is also affected. Early: nephromegaly (enlarged kidneys). Over years: glycogen deposition in glomeruli and tubules → focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) → proteinuria → progressive CKD. The combination of chronic hyperuricaemia, recurrent hyperlactataemia, and glomerulosclerosis makes renal failure a major long-term complication in adults with Von Gierke disease who survived before modern dietary management existed.
G6Pase deficiency in kidney → glycogen accumulation → FSGS → proteinuria → CKD

High-Yield Exam Intelligence
What Trips Students Up — And How to Think Past It

Von Gierke disease appears in exams in several specific ways — as a clinical vignette, as an MCQ about biochemical mechanisms, and heavily in R&A questions about why glucagon fails. Here are the specific traps and how to think through them.

The Most Common Exam Traps
⚠️ Classic Trap 1
"Glucagon doesn't work because the liver has no glycogen"
This is wrong. The liver in Von Gierke is packed with glycogen — more than normal, in fact. Glucagon fails not because there's no glycogen to break down, but because even after glycogen is broken down successfully to G6P, the G6P cannot be converted to free glucose (no G6Pase). Glycogenolysis runs fine. The block is at the final step. Remember: the problem is export, not production.
⚠️ Classic Trap 2
"Von Gierke causes hypoglycaemia because gluconeogenesis is blocked"
Also wrong. Gluconeogenesis runs perfectly normally in Von Gierke — all the enzymes (Pyruvate Carboxylase, PEPCK, FBPase-1) are intact. The liver makes G6P from lactate, amino acids, and glycerol just fine. But all that newly made G6P also cannot exit as free glucose because G6Pase is the final step of gluconeogenesis too. The harder concept — and the exam-favourite point — is that G6Pase is the bottleneck for BOTH glycogenolysis AND gluconeogenesis simultaneously.
⚠️ Classic Trap 3
"High insulin causes the hypoglycaemia"
Wrong again. Insulin is actually low in Von Gierke — the pancreas correctly senses low blood glucose and reduces insulin secretion. This is an important distinction from hyperinsulinism (e.g. insulinoma, congenital hyperinsulinism), which also causes hypoglycaemia but has high insulin. In Von Gierke, the hypoglycaemia is purely from hepatic glucose export failure, not from insulin excess. Ketones may also be reduced (though not always absent), because insulin suppresses lipolysis — but in Von Gierke, even during hypoglycaemia, the massive triglyceride load provides some substrate for ketogenesis. This is a nuanced point that high-level questions exploit.
💡 Key Insight 1
The paradox of abundant substrate and starvation
Von Gierke is a disease of paradox. The liver is bursting with glucose-containing molecules (glycogen, G6P) — yet the blood is starving of glucose. The liver is producing glucose at maximum rate (glycogenolysis + gluconeogenesis both running) — yet blood glucose falls. This paradox is exactly what the blocked final step creates: production and storage are normal or even supranormal, but export is zero. Questions that present "the liver is trying to produce glucose but blood glucose is low" are pointing directly at this paradox.
💡 Key Insight 2
Why lactic acidosis rises paradoxically with glucagon
When glucagon is given, it stimulates glycogenolysis — glycogen → G6P. That G6P floods into glycolysis → pyruvate → lactate. More glucagon = more G6P = more lactate. Lactate rises when glucagon is given — the opposite of what you'd expect. This is the biochemical basis of the glucagon test finding: blood glucose barely moves, but blood lactate rises. The test is positive (suggesting Von Gierke) when you see this pattern. It's exploited in R&A questions: "Glucagon administration worsens lactic acidosis in Von Gierke disease BECAUSE G6Pase deficiency means G6P produced by glycogenolysis cannot be exported as glucose and instead overflows into glycolysis."
⚖️ Comparison Trap
Von Gierke vs Cori Disease (GSD Type III)
Both cause hepatomegaly and hypoglycaemia. The key differences:

Von Gierke: Glucagon unresponsive (G6Pase blocked — both glycogenolysis AND gluconeogenesis fail to export glucose). Lactic acidosis prominent. Hypertriglyceridaemia severe. Gluconeogenesis intact but useless. Hypoglycaemia severe (no glucose export at all).

Cori Disease: Glucagon partially responsive (glycogenolysis can release some outer-chain glucose before hitting limit dextrin, AND gluconeogenesis is fully intact and can export glucose via G6Pase). Lactic acidosis mild. Hypoglycaemia milder. Improves with age. If an exam question says "hypoglycaemia responds to protein feeding" — that's Cori, not Von Gierke. Protein → amino acids → gluconeogenesis → G6P → G6Pase → free glucose. This works in Cori (G6Pase intact). It doesn't work in Von Gierke (G6Pase absent).
⚖️ Comparison Trap
Von Gierke vs Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase Deficiency
FBPase-1 deficiency also causes fasting hypoglycaemia and lactic acidosis and is triggered by fasting. The key difference: in FBPase-1 deficiency, glycogenolysis is fully intact. The liver can break down glycogen normally and export glucose via G6Pase. Hypoglycaemia only occurs once glycogen is depleted (longer fasting required). Glucagon works normally in FBPase-1 deficiency — it stimulates glycogenolysis, G6P is produced, and G6Pase exports it as free glucose. In Von Gierke, glucagon cannot work even when glycogen is full, because G6Pase itself is missing. This is the discriminating test: glucagon response distinguishes Von Gierke (no response) from FBPase-1 deficiency (normal response, at least early).
⚖️ Comparison Trap
GSD Type Ia vs Type Ib
Type Ia: G6Pase enzyme itself is absent (G6PC gene mutation). Type Ib: The transporter that moves G6P into the ER lumen (G6PT, encoded by SLC37A4) is absent — G6Pase is present but cannot access its substrate. Clinical presentation is identical — same hypoglycaemia, same hepatomegaly, same lactic acidosis, same metabolic profile. The distinguishing feature of Type Ib is neutropenia and neutrophil dysfunction — because neutrophils also use G6PT, and without it they cannot generate enough energy for their function. Type Ib patients have recurrent bacterial infections and inflammatory bowel disease-like symptoms. If an exam gives you Von Gierke features PLUS recurrent infections or neutropenia — think Type Ib.
R&A Framework
How to Think Through Von Gierke R&A Questions

R&A questions on Von Gierke typically test one of three things: (1) whether glucagon works and why, (2) why each metabolic abnormality occurs, or (3) why the disease is more severe than other GSDs that also impair glycogenolysis.

Framework for Assertion evaluation: Ask yourself — is this statement describing a consequence of G6Pase deficiency? If yes, is the consequence a direct result (G6P accumulation) or a secondary overflow effect (lactic acidosis, hypertriglyceridaemia, hyperuricaemia)?

Framework for Reason evaluation: Does the reason correctly identify the biochemical mechanism? Watch for reasons that are true statements but explain the assertion by a different mechanism than the correct one. For example: "Hypoglycaemia occurs in Von Gierke disease BECAUSE insulin levels are high." The assertion (hypoglycaemia) is true; the reason (high insulin) is false — insulin is actually low. Option C.

The trickiest R&A pattern: Both assertion and reason are true, but the reason explains a different part of the pathophysiology. Example: "The liver is enlarged in Von Gierke disease BECAUSE glycogenolysis is impaired." Both are technically true — the liver is enlarged (assertion ✓) and glycogenolysis is somewhat impaired in the sense that its product cannot be exported (reason ✓). But is the reason the correct explanation? Not quite — the enlargement is due to G6P accumulation → glycogen synthesis → glycogen accumulation, not directly from impaired glycogenolysis. This is Option B territory — both true, reason not the correct explanation.

Diagnosis
How Von Gierke Disease Is Diagnosed

Diagnosis combines clinical suspicion, metabolic investigations, imaging, and genetic confirmation. The sequence below reflects how it typically unfolds in practice.

Diagnostic Pathway
1
Clinical Suspicion
Infant presenting with hypoglycaemia after a short fast (2–4 hours) plus massive hepatomegaly. The combination is highly suggestive. Doll-like facies and protuberant abdomen in a toddler with documented hypoglycaemia should prompt immediate investigation. A family history (autosomal recessive — parents may be consanguineous or from specific ethnic groups with higher carrier rates) increases suspicion.
2
Metabolic Screen (Fasting Studies)
During a controlled fast under medical supervision: blood glucose (falls rapidly), blood lactate (elevated — often >5 mmol/L), uric acid (elevated), triglycerides (elevated), insulin (low, appropriate), ketones (low to absent despite hypoglycaemia — this is an important clue; most other hypoglycaemic states have high ketones). The pattern of hypoketotic hypoglycaemia with lactic acidosis and hypertriglyceridaemia is highly characteristic.
3
Glucagon Stimulation Test
Give 1 mg glucagon IM after an overnight fast (only when the child is safe to fast — often requires IV glucose running). Measure blood glucose and lactate at 15, 30, 45, 60 minutes. In Von Gierke: glucose rises <1 mmol/L; lactate rises. In normal individuals: glucose rises >1.5 mmol/L. This test is diagnostic when positive but must be performed safely with glucose backup.
4
Imaging
Liver ultrasound confirms hepatomegaly and may show increased echogenicity (from glycogen and fat deposition). Kidneys are also enlarged. As patients age, ultrasound surveillance is used to monitor for hepatic adenomas (appear in adolescence/adulthood in poorly controlled patients). MRI liver is more sensitive for detecting adenomas and distinguishing them from hepatocellular carcinoma.
5
Genetic Testing (Confirmatory)
Sequencing of the G6PC gene (Type Ia) or SLC37A4 gene (Type Ib) confirms the diagnosis and identifies the specific mutation. This has largely replaced liver biopsy for diagnosis. Genetic testing is also used for family screening and prenatal diagnosis. Liver biopsy was historically used to measure G6Pase enzyme activity directly but is now rarely needed when genetic testing is available.
Management
Treatment — The Goal Is to Bypass the Block

There is no cure currently available for Von Gierke disease (though gene therapy trials are ongoing). Treatment is entirely directed at preventing hypoglycaemia and managing the downstream metabolic consequences. The principle is simple: if you keep blood glucose from ever falling, G6P never builds up to the same degree, and all the downstream cascades are dampened.

Dietary Management — Cornerstone of Treatment
Continuous glucose supply is the principle. In infancy: continuous nasogastric feeds overnight. As children grow: uncooked cornstarch (UCCS) — a slowly digested complex carbohydrate that releases glucose gradually over 4–6 hours, extending the safe fasting window. Cornstarch is given every 3–6 hours (including overnight) mixed into feeds or drinks. It is the cornerstone of outpatient management and has dramatically changed outcomes. High-carbohydrate, low-fat, low-fructose, low-galactose diet. Fructose and galactose are avoided because they are metabolised to F1P and G1P which cannot be exported either — they worsen the metabolic burden.
Managing Metabolic Complications
Hyperuricaemia: Allopurinol (xanthine oxidase inhibitor) to reduce uric acid production. Hypertriglyceridaemia: Dietary fat restriction; fibrates in severe cases; fish oil. Lactic acidosis: Prevention (avoid fasting) is the mainstay; sodium bicarbonate in acute crises. Nephropathy: ACE inhibitors to reduce proteinuria; strict metabolic control slows progression. Anaemia: If present, G-CSF in Type Ib to stimulate neutrophil production.
Monitoring & Surveillance
Regular fasting glucose + lactate to assess metabolic control. Annual liver ultrasound from adolescence for adenoma surveillance. Renal function (urine protein, GFR) annually. Bone density (chronic metabolic acidosis and poor nutrition impair mineralisation). Lipid panel regularly. Metabolic dietitian review every 3–6 months.
Emerging & Future Therapies
Gene therapy: AAV-mediated delivery of the G6PC gene to the liver is in Phase I/II trials and has shown promising results in animal models and early human trials. Liver transplantation corrects the hepatic defect (liver has G6Pase again) and resolves hypoglycaemia and most metabolic abnormalities — but is reserved for severe adenoma disease or hepatocellular carcinoma risk. Renal disease is not corrected by liver transplant (kidney G6Pase remains absent). Extended-release cornstarch formulations improve overnight glucose stability.
For Your Exams
The Five Things You Must Know Cold

1. G6Pase is the final step of BOTH glycogenolysis AND gluconeogenesis. Blocking it means neither pathway can export glucose — that's why the disease is so severe.

2. Glucagon is unresponsive — not because glycogen is absent (it's abundant) but because G6P produced by glycogenolysis cannot be converted to free glucose. Glucagon actually worsens lactic acidosis.

3. The six key features all trace back to G6P accumulation overflowing into different pathways: hypoglycaemia (no export), hepatomegaly (→ glycogen), lactic acidosis (→ glycolysis), hypertriglyceridaemia (→ lipid synthesis), hyperuricaemia (→ PPP + lactate competes), nephromegaly (kidney G6Pase also absent).

4. Insulin is LOW in Von Gierke — the hypoglycaemia is not insulin-driven. This distinguishes it from hyperinsulinism.

5. Type Ib has identical metabolic presentation to Type Ia but adds neutropenia and recurrent infections — because the G6P transporter (not G6Pase itself) is deficient, affecting neutrophil function.
