Gluconeogenesis Mastery
The synthesis of new glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. The cell's answer to fasting — running largely in the liver to keep blood glucose from falling to lethal levels.

Primary Location
Liver (dominant site). Kidney cortex takes over during prolonged fasting and acidosis. Skeletal muscle and brain cannot perform gluconeogenesis — they lack Glucose-6-phosphatase.
Compartments
Unlike glycolysis (entirely cytosolic), gluconeogenesis spans two compartments. Steps 1–2 begin in the mitochondria; the pathway continues in the cytosol from step 3 onward.
Overall Equation (from pyruvate)
2 Pyruvate + 4 ATP + 2 GTP + 2 NADH + 6 H₂O
→ Glucose + 4 ADP + 2 GDP + 6 Pᵢ + 2 NAD⁺
When It Runs
Activated during fasting, starvation, prolonged exercise, low-carbohydrate states. Stimulated by glucagon and cortisol. Inhibited by insulin. The pathway is essentially reciprocally regulated with glycolysis — when one runs, the other slows.
The Core Principle — Why Not Just Reverse Glycolysis?
Seven of glycolysis's ten steps are freely reversible under physiological conditions — gluconeogenesis simply runs those steps in reverse. But three glycolytic reactions are irreversible, catalysed by Hexokinase, PFK-1, and Pyruvate Kinase, each with a large negative ΔG that makes reversal thermodynamically impossible. Gluconeogenesis gets around each of these with a dedicated bypass enzyme (or two). This is the entire conceptual heart of gluconeogenesis — understand the four bypass enzymes and you understand the pathway.
Gluconeogenic Substrates — where they enter the pathway
→ Pyruvate
Lactate — the major substrate during the Cori cycle. Lactate Dehydrogenase converts lactate → pyruvate in the liver. Also: glucogenic amino acids (e.g. Alanine via the glucose-alanine cycle → pyruvate by transamination).
→ OAA
Glucogenic amino acids that enter the TCA cycle as oxaloacetate or are converted to OAA (e.g. Aspartate, Asparagine). Also intermediates that feed OAA via the TCA cycle.
→ DHAP / G3P
Glycerol — released from fat breakdown (lipolysis of triacylglycerols). Glycerol kinase converts glycerol → glycerol-3-phosphate → DHAP, entering at step 5 of the reversed pathway.
→ Succinyl-CoA
Odd-chain fatty acids — beta-oxidation of odd-chain fatty acids produces propionyl-CoA, converted to succinyl-CoA, which enters the TCA cycle and can contribute carbons to OAA and ultimately gluconeogenesis. (Even-chain fatty acids cannot be net gluconeogenic substrates.)

Pyruvate Carboxylase

Explanation
Pyruvate Carboxylase adds CO₂ to pyruvate using biotin as the CO₂ carrier — biotin first picks up the CO₂ (at the cost of one ATP), then delivers it to pyruvate to form oxaloacetate. This is why biotin is an essential cofactor: without it, this enzyme is completely non-functional, and gluconeogenesis from pyruvate and lactate stalls entirely.

The Acetyl-CoA dependence is a beautifully logical regulatory checkpoint. Acetyl-CoA accumulates when fatty acid oxidation is running at high rates (exactly the situation during fasting, when the body is burning fat). This signals: "fat breakdown is active, glucose must be scarce — activate gluconeogenesis." Acetyl-CoA doesn't enter gluconeogenesis itself (even-chain fatty acids cannot be net gluconeogenic), but it acts as the metabolic signal that it's the right time to make glucose from other precursors.

OAA cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane directly. It is either transaminated to aspartate (which can cross) or reduced to malate by mitochondrial Malate Dehydrogenase, crosses as malate, then is reoxidised to OAA in the cytosol. This shuttle also transfers reducing equivalents (NADH) to the cytosol, which are needed for step 6r.

Inter-pathway link: TCA CycleOAA is a TCA cycle intermediate. Pyruvate Carboxylase feeds OAA into both gluconeogenesis (when glucagon is high) and the TCA cycle (anaplerosis — replenishing cycle intermediates). Which direction depends on the cell's energy state. Fatty Acid OxidationAcetyl-CoA from beta-oxidation activates Pyruvate Carboxylase, linking fat breakdown to glucose synthesis during fasting. This is why fatty acid oxidation is a prerequisite for efficient gluconeogenesis from pyruvate.

Explanation
PEPCK simultaneously decarboxylates (removes CO₂) and phosphorylates OAA, using GTP as the phosphate donor rather than ATP — worth noting because GTP is less common as an energy currency and is a distinctive exam feature of this enzyme.

PEPCK is the rate-limiting enzyme of gluconeogenesis — its activity determines the overall pace of the pathway. Critically, its regulation is primarily transcriptional rather than allosteric: glucagon and cortisol increase PEPCK mRNA levels (gene expression), while insulin decreases them. This means hormonal control of gluconeogenesis works on a slower timescale (hours) than the allosteric controls of glycolysis (seconds to minutes).

This is a high-yield exam point: metformin's mechanism includes suppression of hepatic PEPCK expression (among other actions), which is why it reduces hepatic glucose output and lowers fasting blood glucose in type 2 diabetes.

Inter-pathway link: GlycolysisPEPCK produces PEP, which is an intermediate shared with glycolysis. The direction of flow (toward pyruvate or toward glucose) is determined by the regulatory states of Pyruvate Kinase (glycolysis) and PEPCK (gluconeogenesis).

Explanation
FBPase-1 is a simple hydrolase — it breaks the phosphate ester bond with water, releasing free inorganic phosphate. No energy is generated or consumed directly. What makes this enzyme remarkable is its regulatory relationship with PFK-1: they are inhibited and activated by exactly opposite signals. When AMP is high, PFK-1 is activated and FBPase-1 is inhibited — glycolysis runs, gluconeogenesis stops. When ATP is high and AMP is low, PFK-1 is inhibited and FBPase-1 is activated — gluconeogenesis can proceed.

The central regulator coordinating both is Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate: it activates PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1 simultaneously. Insulin raises F-2,6-BP (favouring glycolysis); glucagon lowers it (favouring gluconeogenesis). This reciprocal control at one metabolite is the cell's most elegant mechanism for preventing both pathways from running simultaneously.

Inter-pathway link: Glycolysis / Futile CyclePFK-1 and FBPase-1 are the reciprocal pair most tightly controlled to prevent futile cycling. Their simultaneous operation would continuously convert F6P → F-1,6-BP → F6P, consuming ATP without any productive output.

Explanation
This is the final step — the release of free glucose from the cell into the bloodstream. Glucose-6-phosphatase is embedded in the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, facing the ER lumen, which means G6P must be transported into the ER lumen before it can be dephosphorylated, and the resulting glucose and phosphate are then released.

Its tissue-restricted expression is the defining feature with the most clinical and physiological consequences. The liver and kidney cortex express this enzyme; muscle, brain, heart, and essentially all other tissues do not. This means that while many tissues can produce G6P (from glycogen breakdown or partial gluconeogenesis), only the liver and kidney can complete the process and export free glucose. Muscle glycogen, for example, can fuel muscle glycolysis but cannot contribute to blood glucose — it has no Glucose-6-phosphatase to export it.

This is also the enzyme deficient in Von Gierke disease — the most important clinical correlation for this step.

Inter-pathway link: GlycogenolysisGlycogen breakdown in the liver produces G6P via phosphoglucomutase from G1P. This G6P is then dephosphorylated by Glucose-6-phosphatase for release into blood — the same final step as gluconeogenesis. Pentose Phosphate PathwayG6P is also the entry substrate for the PPP. In tissues without Glucose-6-phosphatase, G6P produced from gluconeogenic precursors must either feed glycolysis or the PPP — it cannot become free glucose.
Regulatory Overview
Gluconeogenesis and glycolysis must never run simultaneously at full speed — doing so would create a futile cycle that burns ATP without accomplishing anything. The cell prevents this through reciprocal regulation at three pairs of opposing enzymes. Understanding these pairs, and the signals that tip the balance one way or the other, is the entire logic of gluconeogenesis regulation. The signals integrate two levels: immediate allosteric control (responding in seconds to metabolite concentrations) and hormonal transcriptional control (responding in hours to the fed/fasted state).
⚠ Futile Cycling — What It Is and Why It Matters
A futile cycle occurs when a biosynthetic and a degradative pathway run simultaneously, converting metabolites in a loop that consumes energy without net chemical work. The classic example in this context: if PFK-1 (glycolysis) converts F6P → F-1,6-BP while FBPase-1 (gluconeogenesis) simultaneously converts F-1,6-BP → F6P, the net result is the hydrolysis of one ATP per cycle — heat generated, no glucose produced or consumed, no useful output.

How the cell prevents it: The key is that F-2,6-BP activates PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1 simultaneously. When glucagon is high (fasting), F-2,6-BP levels fall — PFK-1 activity drops and FBPase-1 is relieved of inhibition. Gluconeogenesis runs; glycolysis slows. When insulin is high (fed), F-2,6-BP rises — PFK-1 is activated, FBPase-1 is inhibited. Glycolysis runs; gluconeogenesis slows. The two pathways are coordinated, not independent.

The F-2,6-BP switch: This single molecule — Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate — is the most important regulator at this step. It is not a glycolytic or gluconeogenic intermediate itself; it exists solely as a regulatory molecule, produced and destroyed by the bifunctional enzyme PFK-2/FBPase-2. When glucagon signals through cAMP → PKA, PKA phosphorylates PFK-2/FBPase-2, activating its phosphatase domain, destroying F-2,6-BP. PFK-1 is released from activation, FBPase-1 is released from inhibition — gluconeogenesis proceeds. Insulin reverses this, restoring F-2,6-BP and shifting back to glycolysis.

The Acetyl-CoA logic: Acetyl-CoA activating Pyruvate Carboxylase is not a coincidence — it's a deliberate metabolic signal. During fasting, fatty acids are being oxidised, generating large amounts of Acetyl-CoA. This Acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to glucose (even-chain fatty acids are not gluconeogenic), but its accumulation signals that fat oxidation is active, which only happens when glucose is genuinely scarce. Activating Pyruvate Carboxylase in response ensures gluconeogenesis turns on precisely when it is needed most.

Because PEPCK regulation is transcriptional, its response to hormonal signals operates on a timescale of hours, not seconds. This distinguishes it from allosteric control of PFK-1 or FBPase-1, which respond in seconds. The clinical implication: after a meal, insulin suppresses PEPCK gene expression — but it takes time for existing PEPCK protein to be degraded. This is one reason why hepatic glucose output takes time to fully suppress after feeding, and why persistent hyperglucagonaemia (as in type 2 diabetes) chronically upregulates PEPCK and contributes to fasting hyperglycaemia.

Von Gierke Disease (Glycogen Storage Disease Type Ia)
Deficient enzyme: Glucose-6-phosphatase (Bypass 4)
Autosomal RecessiveMost Common GSD

Start from what Glucose-6-phosphatase does: it is the final step of both gluconeogenesis and hepatic glycogenolysis, releasing free glucose into the blood. Without it, the liver can make and store G6P — from glucose, from glycogen breakdown, from gluconeogenesis — but it cannot complete the process and export free glucose. The result is severe fasting hypoglycaemia, even after a short fast, because the liver is completely unable to maintain blood glucose between meals.

G6P cannot be released, so it accumulates. This drives glycogen synthesis (more G6P → more glycogen stored), causing massive hepatomegaly. G6P also diverts into glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway, generating excess pyruvate and lactate — lactic acidosis is therefore a cardinal feature. Excess Acetyl-CoA from this flooding of glycolysis leads to increased fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis — hyperlipidaemia and xanthomas follow. Uric acid accumulates because G6P competes with uric acid for renal tubular secretion — hyperuricaemia and gout complete the picture.

Key exam features to anchor: fasting hypoglycaemia + lactic acidosis + hepatomegaly + hyperlipidaemia + hyperuricaemia in a child. Does not respond to glucagon or adrenaline (because both work by releasing glucose from glycogen via G6P — the block is downstream of that point).
Biotin Deficiency → Pyruvate Carboxylase Failure
Affected enzyme: Pyruvate Carboxylase (Bypass 1)
Nutritional / AcquiredRare but Examinable

Pyruvate Carboxylase is absolutely dependent on biotin as its CO₂-carrying prosthetic group. Without biotin, the enzyme cannot function at all — it cannot carboxylate pyruvate, and therefore cannot produce oxaloacetate, and therefore gluconeogenesis from pyruvate and lactate is completely blocked.

The consequence is a combination of fasting hypoglycaemia (gluconeogenesis from amino acids and lactate fails) and lactic acidosis (pyruvate cannot be converted to OAA, accumulates, and is driven to lactate by Lactate Dehydrogenase). Biotin is also a cofactor for other carboxylases — Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase (fatty acid synthesis) and Propionyl-CoA Carboxylase (odd-chain fatty acid metabolism) — so the deficiency has effects beyond gluconeogenesis alone.

Cause: rare dietary deficiency; classically described with excessive raw egg white consumption (avidin in egg white binds biotin irreversibly in the gut). Also seen in patients on prolonged total parenteral nutrition without biotin supplementation.
PEPCK Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: Phosphoenolpyruvate Carboxykinase (Bypass 2)
Autosomal RecessiveVery Rare

PEPCK deficiency is rare but illustrative. Without PEPCK, OAA produced by Pyruvate Carboxylase cannot be converted to PEP — gluconeogenesis stalls at the second step. The result is profound fasting hypoglycaemia, presenting in infancy, with failure to thrive. OAA accumulates in the mitochondria, which disrupts TCA cycle function (OAA is a TCA cycle intermediate) and causes lactic acidosis from backed-up pyruvate.

Because PEPCK is also needed to export carbon from the TCA cycle (anaplerosis in reverse), its deficiency impairs mitochondrial function broadly, leading to hepatomegaly, hypotonia, and in severe cases, rapidly progressive liver failure. Distinguished from Von Gierke disease by the absence of glycogen accumulation and the presence of mitochondrial dysfunction.
Fasting Hypoglycaemia — General Gluconeogenic Failure
Mechanism: failure to maintain blood glucose during fasting
Multiple CausesHigh-Yield Mechanism

Understanding the gluconeogenic pathway makes the causes of fasting hypoglycaemia logical rather than a list to memorise. Blood glucose during fasting depends on two processes: glycogenolysis (using stored glycogen) and gluconeogenesis (making new glucose). Glycogen stores last roughly 12–18 hours — after that, gluconeogenesis must sustain blood glucose entirely.

Any block in gluconeogenesis produces fasting hypoglycaemia with a characteristic delay — the patient is fine initially (glycogenolysis is intact) but develops hypoglycaemia after stores run out. Key causes: alcohol intoxication (ethanol metabolism generates massive NADH, shifting the NAD⁺/NADH ratio so that oxaloacetate is driven to malate and pyruvate is driven to lactate — both gluconeogenic substrates are diverted away), enzyme deficiencies (as above), cortisol or glucagon deficiency (removing the hormonal drive for gluconeogenesis), and substrate deficiency (muscle wasting removing the amino acid supply in severe malnutrition).
