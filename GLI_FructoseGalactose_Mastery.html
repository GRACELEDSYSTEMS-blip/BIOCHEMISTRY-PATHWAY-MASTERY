Fructose & Galactose Metabolism Mastery
Two dietary monosaccharides that enter the body's energy pathways via dedicated routes — both converging into glycolysis or glycogen synthesis, each with clinically important enzyme deficiencies.
Fructose — Sources & Entry
Found in fruit, honey, sucrose (glucose + fructose), and high-fructose corn syrup. Absorbed in the small intestine via GLUT5 (fructose-specific). Primarily metabolised in the liver via fructokinase; minor metabolism in muscle and adipose tissue via hexokinase. Enters glycolysis below PFK-1 — bypassing the main regulatory checkpoint.
Galactose — Sources & Entry
Found primarily in lactose (glucose + galactose) from dairy. Absorbed via SGLT1 and GLUT2. Metabolised mainly in the liver, intestinal mucosa, and RBCs via the Leloir pathway, converting galactose → glucose-1-phosphate → enters glycolysis or glycogen synthesis.
Why These Pathways Matter
Both pathways exist because fructose and galactose cannot directly enter glycolysis at step 1 the way glucose can — they lack the right structural features for the first glycolytic steps. Each requires dedicated enzymes to convert them into glycolytic intermediates. Defects in these enzymes cause the pathway's characteristic clinical disorders.
Convergence Points
Fructose → DHAP + G3P → enters glycolysis at steps 4–6

Galactose → Glucose-1-phosphate → G6P → enters glycolysis at step 1 (post-phosphorylation), or enters glycogen synthesis
⭐ The Critical Regulatory Implication of Fructose Metabolism
Glucose enters glycolysis at step 1 (Hexokinase) and is controlled at step 3 by PFK-1 — the major rate-limiting enzyme that acts as the cell's energy sensor. Fructose, when metabolised in the liver via Fructokinase and Aldolase B, enters glycolysis as DHAP and G3P — below PFK-1. This means fructose bypasses the main regulatory checkpoint entirely. The liver cannot slow down fructose metabolism when energy is abundant in the same way it can throttle glucose. This unregulated entry leads to: excess Acetyl-CoA → de novo lipogenesis → elevated triglycerides and VLDL, contributing to metabolic syndrome, fatty liver disease, and dyslipidaemia with high fructose intake.

Regulation
Fructokinase has no allosteric regulation — it runs at a rate determined purely by fructose availability. This is the biochemical root of fructose's problematic metabolic effects at high intake: there is no feedback mechanism to slow Fructokinase when the liver has enough energy, unlike glucose metabolism which is tightly controlled at PFK-1.

Deficiency of Fructokinase causes Essential Fructosuria — a benign, asymptomatic condition where fructose accumulates in blood and spills into urine (fructosuria). Since Fructose-1-phosphate is never formed, none of the downstream toxic accumulation occurs.

Inter-pathway link: GlycolysisFructose-1-phosphate cannot directly enter glycolysis. It must first be cleaved by Aldolase B (step F2) into DHAP and glyceraldehyde, which then enter glycolysis at steps 4–6.

Explanation
Aldolase B cleaves Fructose-1-phosphate into dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and D-glyceraldehyde (not glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate). The DHAP can directly enter glycolysis at step 4. The glyceraldehyde must first be phosphorylated to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate by Triokinase (step F3) before it can proceed.

Why Aldolase B deficiency is dangerous: when Aldolase B is absent, Fructose-1-phosphate accumulates massively in liver cells. F-1-P traps inorganic phosphate, depleting the cell's phosphate pool. This inhibits Phosphorylase and glycogen breakdown, and critically sequesters Pi away from oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis. Simultaneously, the liver cell accumulates a toxic compound it cannot process, directly damaging hepatocytes.

Inter-pathway link: Glycolysis Step 4DHAP produced here is identical to the DHAP produced by Aldolase in glycolysis step 4. It feeds directly into the payoff phase of glycolysis via Triose Phosphate Isomerase.

Explanation
Phosphorylates glyceraldehyde at C3 to produce glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, which directly enters glycolysis at step 6 (GAPDH). This is the final step connecting fructose to the glycolytic payoff phase. Together with DHAP from step F2, both 3-carbon fragments are now in the glycolytic stream.

Note the net ATP cost of the liver fructose route: steps F1 and F3 each consume one ATP, meaning two ATPs are invested before glycolysis begins — similar to glucose's 2-ATP investment in the glycolytic investment phase. However, since the products enter below PFK-1, the cell proceeds directly to the payoff phase without the regulatory gatekeeping that glucose faces.
Fructose in Muscle & Adipose Tissue — Alternative Route
In muscle and adipose tissue, Hexokinase phosphorylates fructose at C6 (not C1), producing Fructose-6-phosphate directly — which enters glycolysis at step 2. However, Hexokinase has a much lower affinity for fructose than for glucose (fructose is a poor substrate), so this route is minor under normal dietary conditions. It only becomes significant when very large amounts of fructose reach peripheral tissues, which rarely occurs because the liver captures most dietary fructose on first pass. This route does not produce F-1-P and cannot cause HFI-like toxicity.

Important exam distinction from classic galactosaemia.

The key enzyme of classic galactosaemia. GALT transfers the uridyl group from UDP-Glucose to Galactose-1-phosphate, producing Glucose-1-phosphate (which enters glycolysis) and UDP-Galactose (which feeds step G3). Deficiency causes Gal-1-P and galactitol to accumulate with devastating multi-system consequences.

Product
Glucose-1-phosphate + UDP-Galactose
Coenzyme
UDP (uridine diphosphate) — sugar carrier
Reaction Type
Uridyltransfer (group transfer)
Explanation
GALT is a transferase — it swaps the uridyl-glucose group from UDP-Glucose onto Galactose-1-phosphate. The products are Glucose-1-phosphate (which Phosphoglucomutase converts to G6P for glycolysis) and UDP-Galactose (which feeds the next step for recycling the UDP carrier).

Why GALT deficiency is so serious: both Galactose-1-phosphate and galactitol accumulate. Gal-1-P is directly toxic to hepatocytes (causing liver damage → jaundice, cirrhosis), brain cells (causing intellectual disability), and the lens (cataracts). Galactitol accumulates in the lens. Together they produce the classic galactosaemia triad of jaundice, cataracts, and intellectual disability in a newborn presenting within days of lactose feeding.

Inter-pathway link: Glycogen SynthesisGlucose-1-phosphate produced here can be converted to UDP-Glucose (by UTP:G1P Uridylyltransferase) and incorporated into glycogen — providing an alternative fate for galactose-derived carbon beyond glycolysis.

Explanation
Converts UDP-Galactose back to UDP-Glucose by inverting the configuration at C4 — an epimerisation reaction. This regenerates the UDP-Glucose consumed in step G2, making the UDP carrier available for the next cycle. NAD⁺ is used transiently as part of the reaction mechanism (oxidation then reduction at C4) but is not consumed net.

This step is also important for galactose biosynthesis — when the body needs galactose for glycoprotein or glycolipid synthesis (even in people who have never consumed dairy), GALE can run in reverse, converting UDP-Glucose to UDP-Galactose. This is why classic galactosaemia patients can still synthesise galactose-containing glycoproteins, albeit imperfectly.

GALE deficiency: a rare, usually milder form of galactosaemia. In the peripheral (benign) form, only RBCs are affected — not clinically significant. In the generalised form, it resembles classic galactosaemia.

Phosphoglucomutase converts G1P → G6P, which then either enters glycolysis or (in liver) is dephosphorylated for blood glucose release.
Regulatory Overview
These pathways have relatively limited allosteric regulation compared to glycolysis — the critical regulatory story here is what these pathways bypass (fructose) and substrate-driven flux (galactose). The major regulatory concept is fructose's circumvention of PFK-1, and the hormonal/nutritional control of the enzymes involved.
Fructose Metabolism Regulation
Key concept: bypasses PFK-1 · Fructokinase has no allosteric regulation · Insulin-responsive at expression level
Fructokinase has essentially no allosteric regulation — it is not inhibited by its product (Fructose-1-phosphate) and is not sensitive to the cell's energy status (ATP:ADP ratio). This is the fundamental problem with high fructose intake: the liver processes fructose at a rate proportional to its concentration, regardless of the cell's energetic needs.

Consequences of unregulated fructose metabolism:
1. Rapid conversion to Acetyl-CoA and glycerol-3-phosphate → excessive triglyceride synthesis → non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and elevated VLDL
2. Uric acid production — the rapid phosphorylation of fructose by Fructokinase depletes ATP and raises AMP → AMP is degraded to uric acid → hyperuricaemia and gout
3. Unregulated entry into glycolysis at the triose level drives excess pyruvate → Acetyl-CoA → lipogenesis

Hormonal modulation: Insulin upregulates expression of hepatic enzymes involved in fructose metabolism, and promotes lipogenic use of the resulting Acetyl-CoA. High-fructose diets in the context of hyperinsulinaemia (insulin resistance) are particularly lipogenic.
Galactose Metabolism Regulation
Primarily substrate-driven · GALT is the key enzyme · UDP-glucose availability limits flux
Galactose metabolism via the Leloir pathway is primarily substrate-driven — flux through the pathway depends on the availability of galactose (dietary lactose) and UDP-Glucose (the galactose acceptor in step G2). There is no major allosteric regulatory mechanism comparable to PFK-1 in glycolysis.

UDP-Glucose availability is the practical rate-limiter: GALT requires UDP-Glucose as the acceptor, and if UDP-Glucose is depleted (e.g. high galactose load relative to UDP-Glucose synthesis), Gal-1-P begins to accumulate. This is the biochemical situation that leads to toxicity in classic galactosaemia patients — even small amounts of dietary galactose overwhelm the remaining enzyme capacity.

Developmental regulation: Lactase (intestinal galactosidase that cleaves lactose into glucose + galactose) is high at birth and declines after weaning in most human populations (lactase non-persistence / adult hypolactasia). This means galactose exposure naturally decreases as infants move away from breast milk — which is protective in galactosaemia patients once the diagnosis is made and lactose is excluded from the diet.
⚕️ The clinical correlations here are among the most commonly tested in first-year biochemistry. The fructosuria comparison and the galactosaemia severity spectrum are high-yield for both MCQs and clinical scenario questions.
Essential Fructosuria vs Hereditary Fructose Intolerance — The Critical Comparison
Both involve fructose metabolism — separated by which enzyme is deficient and what accumulates
High-Yield Exam Comparison

This is the most commonly tested distinction in fructose metabolism. The two conditions sound similar but are clinically worlds apart.

Why HFI causes hypoglycaemia: Fructose-1-phosphate accumulation depletes inorganic phosphate (Pi). Without Pi, Glycogen Phosphorylase cannot release glucose from glycogen (the reaction requires Pi as a substrate). Gluconeogenesis is also impaired because Pi depletion disrupts oxidative phosphorylation, reducing ATP for gluconeogenic reactions. The liver cannot mobilise glucose from any source — profound hypoglycaemia follows every fructose exposure.
Classic Galactosaemia — GALT Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: Galactose-1-phosphate Uridyltransferase (GALT · Step G2)
Autosomal RecessiveNewborn Screening TargetMedical Emergency in Neonates

Classic galactosaemia presents in a neonate who appears well at birth but deteriorates within days of milk feeding (breast milk or standard formula both contain lactose → galactose). The biochemical chain: dietary galactose → Galactokinase produces Gal-1-P → GALT absent → Gal-1-P cannot be converted → accumulates massively → toxicity.

Two toxic compounds:
— Galactose-1-phosphate: directly toxic to liver, brain, and kidneys. In liver → hepatocellular damage → jaundice, elevated transaminases, progressive cirrhosis. In brain → intellectual disability, speech delay, cerebral atrophy.
— Galactitol: galactose reduced by Aldose Reductase (the same enzyme involved in diabetic complications). Accumulates in the lens → osmotic swelling → cataracts. Also accumulates in the brain, contributing to neurological damage.

Classic triad: jaundice + cataracts + intellectual disability in a neonate exposed to lactose.

Additional features: E. coli sepsis is classically associated — galactosaemia impairs neutrophil function, predisposing to overwhelming neonatal sepsis. Renal tubular disease (Fanconi syndrome — aminoaciduria, phosphaturia, glycosuria) from Gal-1-P accumulation in renal tubular cells.

Treatment: Immediate elimination of all galactose from the diet (galactose-free formula, lifelong avoidance of dairy and lactose-containing foods). Early treatment prevents liver disease and worsening of cataracts, but neurological outcomes and ovarian failure (premature ovarian insufficiency in females — XX gonadal dysgenesis) often persist despite treatment, suggesting intrauterine galactose toxicity or ongoing endogenous galactose production.

Diagnosis: Newborn screening detects elevated Gal-1-P or absent GALT activity in RBCs. Confirmatory enzyme assay and gene sequencing.
Galactokinase Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: Galactokinase (Step G1)
Autosomal RecessiveMilder than Classic Galactosaemia

When Galactokinase is absent, galactose cannot be phosphorylated to Gal-1-P. Instead, galactose is reduced by Aldose Reductase to galactitol, which accumulates — especially in the lens. The sole significant clinical manifestation is bilateral cataracts, appearing in infancy or early childhood.

There is no liver disease, no intellectual disability, and no sepsis risk — because Gal-1-P never forms, the toxic accumulation of classic galactosaemia does not occur. Galactokinase deficiency is the mildest form of galactosaemia. Treatment is dietary galactose restriction. This condition is an important exam contrast to GALT deficiency — same sugar, very different outcome depending on which enzyme is deficient and therefore which compound accumulates.
UDP-Galactose-4-Epimerase (GALE) Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: UDP-Galactose-4-Epimerase (Step G3)
Autosomal RecessiveVariable Severity

GALE deficiency exists on a spectrum. The peripheral (benign) form — the most common — affects only red blood cells and is clinically silent, discovered incidentally on newborn screening. The generalised form is rare but serious, resembling classic galactosaemia in presentation (jaundice, liver disease, intellectual disability), because Gal-1-P accumulates when GALE cannot regenerate UDP-Glucose for the GALT reaction.

Paradoxically, patients with generalised GALE deficiency require a small amount of dietary galactose — unlike classic galactosaemia patients — because GALE is also responsible for synthesising UDP-Galactose for glycoprotein and glycolipid synthesis. Without any dietary galactose, they cannot make these essential molecules.
