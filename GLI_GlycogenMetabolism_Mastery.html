Glycogen Metabolism Mastery
The storage and mobilisation of glucose. Glycogen is the cell's glucose reserve — rapidly built after meals and rapidly broken down during fasting or exercise. Understanding glycogen metabolism means understanding how the body manages blood glucose between meals.

Glycogen Structure — What You Need to Know
α-1,4 Linkages
Form the straight-chain backbone. Glucose residues joined C1 to C4. Each linear chain contains 12–14 glucose residues. Glycogen Phosphorylase cleaves these bonds during breakdown.
α-1,6 Linkages (Branch Points)
Create the branching structure. Occur every 8–10 residues along the chain. Created by Branching Enzyme during synthesis; require Debranching Enzyme for removal during breakdown.
Glycogenin — The Primer
A self-glucosylating protein at the core (reducing end) of glycogen. Adds the first 8 glucose residues to its own tyrosine residue. Glycogen Synthase cannot initiate chains — it can only extend existing ones. Glycogenin provides the obligatory primer.
Why Branching Matters
More branches = more non-reducing ends = more sites for simultaneous Phosphorylase and Synthase action = faster mobilisation and synthesis. Also increases water solubility of glycogen. Branching is a functional design, not just structural complexity.
Liver vs Muscle — Different Purposes, Different Regulations
🔵 Liver Glycogen
Purpose: Maintain blood glucose for other tissues
Storage: ~100g (6–8% of liver weight)
Key enzyme: Glucose-6-phosphatase — dephosphorylates G6P → free glucose → bloodstream
Trigger: Glucagon (fasting), epinephrine
Allosteric: Glucose directly inhibits Phosphorylase
Replenished: After meals via Glucokinase (high Km)
🟢 Muscle Glycogen
Purpose: Fuel for muscle work only
Storage: ~300g (1–2% of muscle mass)
No Glucose-6-phosphatase — G6P cannot leave muscle, goes directly to glycolysis
Trigger: Epinephrine, Ca²⁺ (during contraction)
Allosteric: AMP activates, ATP/G6P inhibit Phosphorylase
Cannot contribute to blood glucose
Importance of Glycogen Metabolism
Blood glucose buffer: liver glycogen yields free glucose to maintain blood glucose between meals

Intracellular G6P supply: for glycolysis → ATP in muscle/brain

PPP substrate: G6P feeds pentose phosphate pathway → NADPH + ribose derivatives

UDP-Glucose (Uridine Diphosphate Glucose) is the activated form — the high-energy bond between glucose and UDP provides the thermodynamic driving force for glycogen synthesis. The release of pyrophosphate (PPi), which is immediately hydrolysed to 2 inorganic phosphates by pyrophosphatase, makes the overall reaction essentially irreversible and pulls it strongly forward.

This principle — using a nucleoside triphosphate to activate a metabolite — appears repeatedly in biosynthesis (UDP-galactose in galactose metabolism, CTP in phospholipid synthesis). The pattern is always the same: activation costs one NTP, and PPi hydrolysis drives the reaction to completion.

Glycogenin provides the obligatory primer — it is a homodimer (two identical subunits) that auto-glucosylates itself, attaching glucose to a specific tyrosine-OH residue using UDP-Glucose. Each subunit adds 8 glucose residues to its partner subunit, building the primer chain Glycogen Synthase then extends.

Glycogenin remains covalently attached at the core of the glycogen molecule throughout its life — it is the structural anchor of every glycogen granule. This is why glycogen has a single reducing end (attached to glycogenin) and many non-reducing ends (where Glycogen Synthase adds glucose and Phosphorylase removes it).

The clinical implication: a patient deficient in glycogenin (very rare) cannot synthesise glycogen at all. This is conceptually distinct from Glycogen Synthase deficiency (GSD Type 0), where the primer exists but cannot be elongated.

Glycogen Synthase exists in two interconvertible forms: Synthase a (dephosphorylated, active) and Synthase b (phosphorylated, inactive). This is the exact opposite of Glycogen Phosphorylase — phosphorylation activates Phosphorylase but inactivates Synthase. This reciprocal covalent regulation ensures synthesis and breakdown cannot both run simultaneously at full speed.

Glucose-6-phosphate is a key allosteric activator: even the phosphorylated (b) form can be partially activated by high G6P, providing a direct link between glucose availability and glycogen synthesis. When G6P is high (glucose abundant), glycogen synthesis proceeds regardless of the phosphorylation state.

Branching Enzyme takes a segment of 6–7 glucose residues from the end of a chain (which must already be at least 11 residues long) and reattaches it via an α-1,6 bond to a glucose residue on the interior of the chain, creating a new branch point. The new branch must be at least 4 residues away from an existing branch.

The functional significance: each new branch point creates two new non-reducing ends where Glycogen Synthase can add and Glycogen Phosphorylase can remove glucose. This exponential increase in available ends is what gives glycogen its remarkably fast synthesis and mobilisation rates — the molecule is designed for rapid parallel action, not sequential processing.

Andersen Disease (GSD IV)Branching Enzyme deficiency causes Andersen disease (GSD Type IV). Glycogen lacks proper branching — it accumulates as abnormal long-chain polysaccharide (amylopectin-like), which triggers a foreign body reaction in the liver → cirrhosis.

Why phosphorolysis instead of hydrolysis? Using Pᵢ instead of water directly produces Glucose-1-phosphate, which is already phosphorylated and trapped inside the cell. If hydrolysis were used, free glucose would be produced and would have to be re-phosphorylated by Hexokinase at the cost of 1 ATP. Phosphorolysis saves that ATP — an elegant metabolic economy.

PLP (Vitamin B₆) as cofactor: Pyridoxal phosphate is covalently bound to Glycogen Phosphorylase and participates in the catalytic mechanism. This is one of the rare cases where a water-soluble vitamin acts as a prosthetic group (permanently bound) rather than a dissociable coenzyme.

Product of Activity 2

The sequence at every branch point: Glycogen Phosphorylase degrades the chain down to 4 residues from the α-1,6 branch. Then Debranching Enzyme acts:

Step 1 (Transferase): moves 3 of the 4 stub residues to the non-reducing end of another chain, extending it. This creates a new substrate for Phosphorylase and leaves a single glucose residue still attached via α-1,6 bond.

Step 2 (Glucosidase): hydrolyses (using water) the remaining α-1,6-linked glucose, releasing it as free glucose (not Glucose-1-phosphate). This free glucose bypasses Phosphoglucomutase and enters the blood or glycolysis directly.

The two activities are on separate sites of the same polypeptide chain — which is why Cori disease (GSD Type III), caused by Debranching Enzyme deficiency, results in abnormal glycogen with very short outer chains and accumulation of limit dextrin (the stump glycogen left after Phosphorylase has run to its 4-residue limit).

G6P produced from glycogenolysis (or gluconeogenesis) in the liver is dephosphorylated by Glucose-6-phosphatase to free glucose, which exits the hepatocyte via GLUT2 into the bloodstream. This is the final step in the liver's glucose-releasing function.

In muscle, the same G6P is retained — it cannot leave, and it enters glycolysis at Step 2 (Phosphoglucose Isomerase) to generate ATP for muscular work. The muscle is acting purely for its own fuel needs, not for blood glucose maintenance.

Glucagon and adrenaline have no effect because the final step to release glucose is blocked.
Regulatory Overview
Glycogen metabolism is regulated at two key enzymes — Glycogen Phosphorylase (rate-limiting for breakdown) and Glycogen Synthase (rate-limiting for synthesis) — and by three types of regulation: allosteric (immediate, seconds), covalent modulation (phosphorylation/dephosphorylation, minutes), and hormonal (transcriptional + signalling cascade, minutes to hours). The fundamental principle is reciprocal control: the signals that activate breakdown simultaneously inactivate synthesis, and vice versa, so both processes never run at full speed simultaneously.
⚡ The Hormonal Cascade — Glucagon/Epinephrine → Glycogen Breakdown
Glucagon (liver) / Epinephrine (liver + muscle)
Hormones bind to G-protein coupled receptors on cell surface. Glucagon acts primarily in liver; epinephrine acts in both liver and muscle. In muscle, epinephrine also raises Ca²⁺ (independent route).
Adenylyl Cyclase activated → cAMP rises
G-protein (Gαs) activates Adenylyl Cyclase → converts ATP → cyclic AMP (cAMP). cAMP is the second messenger that amplifies the hormone signal enormously — one hormone molecule triggers production of many cAMP molecules.
cAMP activates Protein Kinase A (PKA)
cAMP binds to regulatory subunits of PKA, releasing and activating the catalytic subunits. PKA then phosphorylates multiple downstream targets simultaneously — this is the signal amplification step.
PKA phosphorylates Phosphorylase Kinase → ACTIVE
Phosphorylase Kinase is itself a regulated enzyme. PKA phosphorylates and activates it. In muscle, Ca²⁺ also independently activates Phosphorylase Kinase (via its calmodulin subunit) — this links muscle contraction directly to glycogen breakdown without needing the hormone cascade.
Phosphorylase Kinase: ACTIVE
Phosphorylase Kinase phosphorylates Glycogen Phosphorylase b → Phosphorylase a
Phosphorylase Kinase phosphorylates Ser14 on each subunit of Glycogen Phosphorylase b, converting it to the fully active Phosphorylase a form. Glycogen breakdown accelerates dramatically.
Glycogen Phosphorylase: ACTIVE (a form)
PKA also phosphorylates Glycogen Synthase a → Synthase b (INACTIVE)
Simultaneously, PKA directly phosphorylates Glycogen Synthase, inactivating it. This is the reciprocal control — the same kinase cascade that turns on breakdown turns off synthesis. The cell cannot break down and synthesise glycogen simultaneously at full speed.
Glycogen Synthase: INACTIVE (b form)
🟢 Insulin Reversal — Activating Synthesis, Inhibiting Breakdown
Insulin → activates Protein Phosphatase 1 (PP1)
Insulin signals (via PI3K/Akt pathway) activate Protein Phosphatase 1. PP1 is the key phosphatase that reverses all the phosphorylation events in the cascade.
PP1 dephosphorylates Phosphorylase Kinase → inactive
Removes the activating phosphate from Phosphorylase Kinase, shutting down the activation cascade upstream.
Phosphorylase Kinase: INACTIVE
PP1 dephosphorylates Phosphorylase a → Phosphorylase b (less active)
Glycogen breakdown slows. In the liver, glucose binding to Phosphorylase a also promotes its dephosphorylation — a direct allosteric feedback.
Glycogen Phosphorylase: LESS ACTIVE (b form)
PP1 dephosphorylates Glycogen Synthase b → Synthase a (ACTIVE)
Glycogen synthesis is restored. Combined with the rise in G6P from glucose influx (which allosterically activates Synthase even in the b form), glycogen synthesis runs at maximum rate in the fed state.
Glycogen Synthase: ACTIVE (a form)

The liver-muscle difference reflects their different purposes. The liver monitors blood glucose directly — free glucose entering hepatocytes allosterically inhibits Phosphorylase a, telling the liver "glucose is now available, stop releasing more." The muscle monitors its own energy state — AMP rises when ATP is consumed rapidly during contraction, activating Phosphorylase b without needing the full hormone cascade. Ca²⁺ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum during muscle contraction provides an additional trigger, linking the act of contraction directly to fuel mobilisation.

Glucose-6-phosphate is the most important allosteric activator. When G6P is high, even the phosphorylated (less active) b form of Glycogen Synthase can be partially activated — meaning that when glucose is genuinely abundant, glycogen synthesis proceeds even if the hormonal state hasn't yet fully shifted. This provides a rapid, direct link between glucose availability and glycogen storage without waiting for the full insulin signalling cascade.

GSD Type 0 — Glycogen Synthase Deficiency
Deficient enzyme: Glycogen Synthase (Step S5) · Liver
Autosomal RecessiveNo glycogen accumulation

Type 0 is the conceptual mirror image of all other GSDs. Every other GSD causes glycogen to accumulate abnormally. Type 0 means almost no glycogen is made at all — the liver has essentially no glucose reserve to draw on between meals.

The consequence is a distinctive pattern: fasting hypoglycaemia (no glycogen to break down) combined with elevated ketone bodies (the liver switches to fat oxidation to generate fuel, producing ketones at high rate). Paradoxically, because glycogen cannot be stored, blood glucose after a meal is transiently high — postprandial hyperglycaemia — because the glucose cannot be buffered into glycogen.

Exam trap: students who memorise "GSDs cause glycogen accumulation" will miss Type 0. The deficient enzyme is Glycogen Synthase itself — no accumulation occurs because the substrate (UDP-Glucose) is never incorporated. Early death if untreated due to the severity of hypoglycaemic episodes.
GSD Type Ia — Von Gierke Disease
Deficient enzyme: Glucose-6-phosphatase · Liver + Kidney
Autosomal RecessiveMost common GSD

Glucose-6-phosphatase is the final gate for releasing glucose from both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. Without it, G6P accumulates inside hepatocytes and cannot be exported as free glucose.

Trace the consequences: G6P floods glycogen synthesis → massive hepatomegaly (liver swollen with glycogen). G6P also floods glycolysis → excess pyruvate → excess lactate → lactic acidosis. Excess acetyl-CoA → fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis → hypertriglyceridaemia and hypercholesterolaemia. Glucose cannot be released → severe fasting hypoglycaemia even after short fasts. Hyperuricaemia (G6P competes with urate for renal tubular secretion + excess purine breakdown) → gout.

Critical exam point: Glucagon and epinephrine administration do NOT raise blood glucose in Von Gierke disease — both hormones work by promoting glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, both of which produce G6P, but G6P cannot be dephosphorylated without Glucose-6-phosphatase. This is the diagnostic clue separating it from other causes of fasting hypoglycaemia.
GSD Type II — Pompe Disease (Glycogenosis)
Deficient enzyme: Lysosomal Acid α-Glucosidase (GAA / Acid Maltase) · Skeletal + Cardiac muscle
Autosomal RecessiveLysosomal Storage DiseaseEnzyme Replacement Therapy available

Pompe disease is fundamentally different from all other GSDs — it affects the lysosomal degradation of glycogen, not the cytosolic pathway. All cells continuously take up small amounts of glycogen into lysosomes for turnover; GAA is the enzyme that breaks it down there. Without it, glycogen accumulates progressively inside lysosomes across multiple tissues.

Clinical spectrum by age of onset:
— Infantile (classic): presents within first few months of life with massive cardiomegaly (the heart glycogen accumulation is most life-threatening), profound hypotonia, and respiratory failure. Death by age 2 without treatment.
— Juvenile/Adult: primarily skeletal muscle involvement — progressive limb-girdle myopathy, respiratory muscle weakness mimicking muscular dystrophy. No significant cardiac involvement.

Key distinction from other GSDs: Pompe does not cause fasting hypoglycaemia (cytosolic glycogen metabolism is intact). The primary issue is organ damage from lysosomal glycogen accumulation, not metabolic fuel shortage.

Treatment: Alglucosidase alfa (recombinant GAA — enzyme replacement therapy) significantly improves survival in the infantile form.
GSD Type III — Cori Disease (Forbes Disease)
Deficient enzyme: Debranching Enzyme (Amylo-1,6-Glucosidase) · Liver + Skeletal + Cardiac Muscle
Autosomal RecessiveLimit Dextrin Accumulation

Without Debranching Enzyme, Glycogen Phosphorylase degrades chains normally until it reaches 4 residues from each branch point — then it stops (as always). But now Debranching Enzyme cannot remove the branch, so the leftover stub — called limit dextrin — accumulates. This truncated, over-branched glycogen fills the liver and muscle cells.

Why it's milder than Von Gierke: Gluconeogenesis is entirely intact (Glucose-6-phosphatase is present). During fasting, the liver can still make glucose from amino acids and lactate. Hypoglycaemia is milder and tends to improve with age as gluconeogenic capacity develops. However, the two catalytic activities of Debranching Enzyme can be individually deficient — if only the glucosidase activity is absent, the phenotype differs from complete enzyme deficiency.

Presentation: Hepatomegaly (improving with age), muscle weakness (myopathy from muscle glycogen accumulation), and elevated transaminases. In some patients, cardiac involvement occurs. Distinguishable from Von Gierke by the presence of elevated amino acid transaminases and the milder hypoglycaemia that responds to protein feeding (gluconeogenesis intact).
GSD Type IV — Andersen Disease
Deficient enzyme: Branching Enzyme (Amylo-1,4→1,6-Glucantransferase) · Liver + Skeletal Muscle
Autosomal RecessiveAbnormal glycogen structure

Without Branching Enzyme, glycogen chains are extended but never branched — producing a molecule resembling amylopectin (plant starch) with very long, poorly branched chains. This structurally abnormal polysaccharide accumulates in the liver and is treated as a foreign body by the immune system, triggering a chronic inflammatory response.

The result is progressive cirrhosis and hepatosplenomegaly — the liver is attacked by its own immune system in response to the abnormal glycogen. Liver failure typically occurs in early childhood. Myoglobinuria (muscle breakdown products in urine) may be seen from muscle involvement. Unlike most GSDs, hypoglycaemia is not the primary problem — it is the liver destruction that is life-threatening.

Distinguishing feature: Liver biopsy shows PAS-positive (periodic acid-Schiff) inclusions with abnormally structured glycogen. Liver transplant is the only definitive treatment.
GSD Type V — McArdle Disease
Deficient enzyme: Muscle Glycogen Phosphorylase · Skeletal Muscle only
Autosomal RecessiveExercise-induced symptoms

McArdle disease is the pure muscle counterpart to Von Gierke — muscle Glycogen Phosphorylase is absent, but liver Phosphorylase is normal. Muscle cannot break down its own glycogen for fuel during exercise.

Exercise intolerance is the hallmark: patients develop painful muscle cramps, fatigue, and weakness during exercise, especially intense or sustained activity. The "second wind" phenomenon is pathognomonic — if a patient rests briefly during exercise, symptoms transiently improve as circulating glucose and fatty acids catch up to meet energy demand.

Myoglobinuria follows prolonged or intense exercise: muscle damage releases myoglobin into the urine (dark/red urine), which can cause acute kidney injury. Creatine kinase is markedly elevated during attacks.

Critical exam distinction from Von Gierke: Fasting blood glucose is completely normal in McArdle disease — liver Phosphorylase is unaffected. The problem is entirely confined to muscle fuel use during exercise. Blood lactate fails to rise normally during ischaemic forearm exercise test (a classic diagnostic test — normal subjects produce lactate from muscle glycolysis; McArdle patients cannot).
GSD Type VI — Hers Disease
Deficient enzyme: Liver Glycogen Phosphorylase · Liver
Autosomal RecessiveGenerally benign

Hers disease is the liver counterpart of McArdle disease — liver Glycogen Phosphorylase is deficient, impairing hepatic glycogenolysis, while muscle Phosphorylase is normal. Because gluconeogenesis remains intact, the liver can still produce some glucose from amino acids and lactate, so hypoglycaemia is mild and generally manageable with frequent feeding.

Presentation: Hepatomegaly (from glycogen accumulation) is the dominant finding, usually presenting in childhood. Growth retardation may occur. Symptoms typically improve significantly with age as gluconeogenic capacity increases and glycogen stores relatively diminish. Muscle symptoms are absent.

Prognosis: Generally good — most patients lead normal lives with dietary management (frequent carbohydrate feeding to avoid fasting hypoglycaemia). This contrasts sharply with the severe metabolic and organ consequences of Von Gierke, Pompe, and Andersen diseases.
